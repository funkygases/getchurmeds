Section 1 · The Disease

Epilepsy & Seizure Classification

What epilepsy is, how seizures are classified, and the two treatment strategies.
+

Definition

  • Epilepsy — group of chronic CNS disorders characterized by sudden disturbances of sensory, motor, autonomic, or psychic origin.
  • Seizures = transient synchronous discharge of groups of neurons; symptoms depend on location and duration of discharge.
  • Facilitating factors: changes in blood glucose, electrolytes, PaO2, pH, stress, or fatigue may allow epileptogenic foci to spread into previously inhibitory "normal" areas.
  • Status epilepticus — continuous seizure activity OR >1 seizure in succession without return to consciousness between events.

Seizure Classification

Two treatment strategies Monotherapy first. Combination therapy if monotherapy fails. Goal = seizure control with minimal side effects. Refractory to meds → surgical intervention (vagal nerve stimulator, laser ablation, neurosurgical resection).

Section 2 · AED Pharmacology

AED Pharmacokinetics & Pharmacodynamics

Shared PK patterns, enzyme induction, and the general MOA framework.
+

Pharmacokinetics — Class Patterns

  • Most AEDs rely on liver metabolism + hepatic/renal excretion → dosage adjustments needed for liver/kidney dysfunction.
  • Many are highly protein-bound to albumin → can be displaced by other highly protein-bound drugs or by hypoalbuminemia.
  • Many AEDs induce metabolizing enzymes → ↓ efficacy of other drugs metabolized by the same enzymes (oral contraceptives, many anesthetics).
Phenytoin exception — memorize Phenytoin exhibits non-linear saturation (zero-order) kinetics at plasma concentrations >10 mcg/mL — routine plasma monitoring is required. Therapeutic range: 10–20 mcg/mL.

Pharmacodynamics — General MOA

  • MOA not completely understood but generally: seizure control = decreased neuronal excitability via altered ion currents and/or enhanced inhibitory neurotransmission.
  • Primary ions: sodium and calcium (sometimes potassium).
  • Benzodiazepines — ↑ GABA channel opening frequency.
  • Barbiturates — ↑ GABA channel opening duration.
  • Tiagabine — delays reuptake of GABA, prolonging its action.
Pregnancy consideration Seizure control in pregnancy is critical (significant morbidity/mortality) but many AEDs are teratogenic. Folate supplementation lowers teratogenicity risk.

Section 3 · Sodium Channel Stabilizers

Phenytoin, Carbamazepine & Lamotrigine

The sodium channel trio — the most tested drug interactions in this class.
+

Phenytoin

  • Use: partial and generalized seizures; IV also suppresses cardiac dysrhythmias.
  • PK: oral and IV; 90% protein bound; half-life 9–40 hrs; 98% hepatic metabolism. First-order elimination <10 mcg/mL → zero-order >10 mcg/mL.
  • IV rule: administer at ≤5 mg/min; precipitates in solutions with pH <7.8.
  • MOA: regulates Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ transport → ↓ neuronal excitability.
  • Side effects: nystagmus, ataxia, diplopia, vertigo, peripheral neuropathy, gingival hyperplasia, acne, hirsutism, fetal hydantoin syndrome, hyperglycemia (inhibits insulin secretion), megaloblastic anemia, hepatotoxicity.
Anesthesia interaction Patients on phenytoin require increased NDNMB doses — phenytoin increases metabolism of NMBs AND may cause upregulation of acetylcholine receptors.

Carbamazepine

  • Use: partial and generalized seizures; trigeminal and glossopharyngeal neuralgia.
  • PK: oral only; peak 2–6 hrs; 80% protein bound; half-time 8–24 hrs; hepatic metabolism to active metabolites.
  • Key note: induces its own metabolism → may need dose increase 2–4 weeks after starting therapy.
  • Also induces metabolism of: oral contraceptives, valproic acid, corticosteroids, anticoagulants, antipsychotics.
  • Drugs that inhibit carbamazepine metabolism: cimetidine, propoxyphene, diltiazem, verapamil, isoniazid, erythromycin.
  • Side effects: sedation, vertigo, diplopia, N/V; rare: aplastic anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, jaundice, oliguria, HTN, cardiac dysrhythmias.

Lamotrigine

  • Use: partial-onset and generalized seizures.
  • PK: half-life 25 hrs; 50% protein bound.
  • MOA: stabilizes voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
  • Phenytoin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine decrease its half-life by 50%; valproic acid increases it to 60 hours.
  • Side effects: headache, N/V, diplopia, ataxia, tremor, Stevens-Johnson syndrome (rare).

Section 6 · Emergency Management

Status Epilepticus

Step-by-step treatment sequence — airway first, drugs second.
+
Medical emergency Prolonged or rapid recurring seizures. Goal: prompt suppression of seizure activity + support airway & circulation.

Treatment Sequence

Rule out hypoglycemia
(50 mL D50 if present)→ Optimize airway & O2
(intubate if needed)→ Benzodiazepines ±
AEDs→ Monitor ABGs
(bicarb if acidotic)→ Active cooling
(treat hyperthermia)
  • Propofol or thiopental will temporarily stop seizure activity.
  • If benzo not effective → phenytoin, phenobarbital, valproic acid.
  • Monitor ABGs — metabolic acidosis is a common sequalae of continued seizure activity; bicarb may be needed.
  • Active cooling may be needed to treat commonly associated hyperthermia.

Section 7 · The Disease

Parkinson's Disease — Pathophysiology & Goals

Why dopamine matters, the classic triad, and the two treatment strategies.
+
  • Neurodegenerative disorder with loss of dopaminergic fibers normally present in the basal ganglia.
  • Dopamine normally inhibits rate of firing in the extrapyramidal motor system. Depletion → reduced inhibition → unopposed ACh stimulation.

Classic Triad

Skeletal muscle tremor+ Rigidity+ Akinesia
  • Also: facial immobility, pill rolling, diaphragmatic spasms, dementia, depression.
Two treatment strategies 1 — Increase dopamine in basal ganglia (levodopa, dopamine agonists, COMT inhibitors, MAO-B inhibitors).
2 — Decrease acetylcholine effects (anticholinergics).
Failure of pharmacologic therapy → deep brain stimulator (DBS) placement.

Section 8 · Levodopa

Levodopa — The Cornerstone Agent

Mechanism, metabolism, side effects, drug interactions, and why abrupt withdrawal is dangerous.
+

Mechanism

Levodopa crosses BBB→ Aromatic-L-amino-acid decarboxylase converts to dopamine→ Replenishes dopamine in basal ganglia
  • ~80% of an oral dose is rapidly decarboxylated to dopamine in first-pass through liver — this is why it is combined with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor.
  • Half-time: 1–3 hours → requires frequent dosing.
  • Beneficial effect typically diminishes after 5–10 years.
  • Metabolites: most → dopamine, some → norepinephrine and epinephrine; further metabolism yields 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid.
  • Dietary methionine is necessary for continued COMT activity to metabolize excess dopamine.
  • High protein meals should be avoided — amino acids interfere with levodopa transport to the brain.

Side Effects

  • N/V (up to 80%) — reflects dopamine action on the CRTZ; tends to improve with longer therapy. Best treatment: domperidone (does NOT cross BBB).
  • Cardiac: alpha/beta stimulation, flushing, orthostatic hypotension (30%), dysrhythmias.
  • Dyskinesias — abnormal involuntary movements develop in ~50% within 1–4 months; tolerance does NOT develop to this effect.
Abrupt withdrawal — dangerous Sudden discontinuation can cause Parkinsonism-Hyperpyrexia Syndrome: rigidity, pyrexia, autonomic instability, ↓ LOC. Complications: aspiration pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, venous thrombosis, renal failure, death.

Drug Interactions

  • AVOID in Parkinson's: antipsychotics (butyrophenones, phenothiazines), droperidol, metoclopramide — these antagonize dopamine activity.
  • Nonspecific MAOIs — interfere with catecholamine inactivation → exaggerate peripheral effects of levodopa.
  • Anticholinergics — work synergistically with levodopa; however, in large doses may interfere with levodopa GI absorption.
  • Pyridoxine — abolishes efficacy of levodopa by enhancing pyridoxine-dependent decarboxylase metabolism prior to CNS entry.

Section 9 · Levodopa Adjuncts

Peripheral Decarboxylase Inhibitors, COMT Inhibitors & Dopamine Agonists

The drugs that maximize levodopa efficacy and the alternatives that don't need it at all.
+

Peripheral Decarboxylase Inhibitors (Carbidopa / Benserazide)

  • Co-administered with levodopa to prevent peripheral conversion before CNS entry.
  • Do NOT cross the BBB and have no activity when given alone.
  • Reduce peripheral side effects of levodopa but do not affect dyskinesias or psychiatric disturbances.
  • Combinations available: Sinemet (levodopa/carbidopa), Madopar (levodopa/benserazide).

COMT Inhibitors (Tolcapone / Entacapone)

  • COMT partially responsible for peripheral conversion of levodopa → dopamine; inhibition allows more levodopa to reach the brain.
  • Both inhibit COMT in the GI tract → ↑ plasma levodopa/carbidopa by 10–15%.
  • Side effects: dyskinesias, N/V, hepatotoxicity (tolcapone), rhabdomyolysis (tolcapone), orange urine (entacapone).

Synthetic Dopamine Agonists

  • Include: pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine (nonergot); bromocriptine (tetracyclic ergot alkaloid).
  • Do NOT require transformation or facilitated BBB transport — act directly on dopamine receptors.
  • Side effects: hallucinations, hypotension, dyskinesia, pleuropulmonary fibrosis, erythromelalgia (red/edematous/tender extremities), vertigo, N/V.
  • Abrupt discontinuation → Parkinsonism-Hyperpyrexia Syndrome (same as levodopa).

Section 10 · Additional Anti-Parkinson Agents

Anticholinergics, Amantadine & MAO-B Inhibitors

Non-dopaminergic strategies and the MAO-B drug interactions to know.
+

Anticholinergics (Trihexyphenidyl / Benztropine)

  • Blunt excitatory acetylcholine effects; primarily used for tremor.
  • Also reduce excessive salivation; some effect on rigidity and bradykinesia.
  • Side effects: hallucinations, confusion, sedation, mydriasis, cycloplegia, adynamic ileus, urinary retention.

Amantadine

  • Antiviral (influenza A prophylaxis) with Parkinson's benefit by unknown mechanism.
  • May ↑ dopamine release, slow reuptake, promote anticholinergic effects, and antagonize glutamate/NMDA.
  • Better effect on rigidity and bradykinesia than anticholinergics.
  • Side effects: ankle edema, livedo reticularis, confusion, psychosis, potential cardiac failure.

MAO-B Inhibitors (Selegiline, Rasagiline, Safinamide)

  • MAO-B is a principal catabolic pathway for dopamine in the CNS → blockade ↑ dopamine half-time.
  • Selegiline — does NOT affect norepinephrine metabolism → no life-threatening NE potentiation. Side effects: confusion, depression, paranoia, insomnia.
  • Rasagiline and Safinamide — theoretical risk of serotonin syndrome; avoidance of tyramine recommended.

Section 11 · CNS Stimulants

Amphetamine, Doxapram, Methylxanthines & Others

Stimulants, analeptics, and their anesthetic implications.
+

Amphetamine / Methylphenidate

  • Prescribed for ADHD, narcolepsy, obesity. Stimulates release of norepinephrine.
  • Central effects: respiratory stimulation, alertness, ↑ muscle strength, concentration, euphoria.
  • Peripheral effects: ↑ BP, tachy or reflex bradycardia.
Anesthesia implications Acute intoxication → ↑ MAC. Chronic use → ↓ MAC + depleted catecholamine stores.
Hypotension, hemodynamic instability, ↓ organ perfusion under anesthesia → requires direct-acting vasopressors: epinephrine, phenylephrine, or vasopressin.
Methylphenidate side effects include seizures, HTN, tachycardia, stroke, and MI.

Doxapram

  • Analeptic — augments breathing; ↑ tidal volume > ↑ frequency.
  • Large safety margin: 20–40 fold difference between respiratory-stimulating and seizure-producing dose.
  • Continuous infusion needed: 2–3 mg/min; single dose lasts only 5–10 min.
  • Uses: COPD patients on O2 (hypoxic drive dependent), prevent/treat opioid-induced ventilatory depression, post-op respiratory support, treat post-op shivering.
  • Side effects: tachycardia, dysrhythmias, ↑ temp, N/V, HTN.

Methylxanthines (Caffeine, Theophylline, Theobromine)

  • CNS stimulation, diuresis, ↑ myocardial contractility, smooth muscle relaxation.
  • Antagonize adenosine → ↑ catecholamine release; at high doses inhibit phosphodiesterase → ↑ cAMP.
  • Used for primary apnea of prematurity — stimulate medullary respiratory centers, ↑ CO2 sensitivity.
  • Therapeutic range theophylline: 10–20 mcg/mL; peak after 5 mg/kg oral dose at 1–2 hours = 10 mcg/mL.
  • Drug interactions: carbamazepine/rifampin ↑ theophylline breakdown; cimetidine/erythromycin inhibit it.
  • Ketamine may decrease seizure threshold of theophylline.
  • Theophylline antagonizes effects of NDNMBs.
  • Caffeine 75–300 mg PO can treat post-dural puncture headache.
  • Side effects: nervousness, tremors, N/V, dysrhythmias, tachycardia, seizures (toxicity or rapid IV).

Other CNS Stimulants

  • Almitrine — acts on carotid chemoreceptors → ↑ minute ventilation, ↑ PaO2, ↓ PaCO2. May prevent hypoxia during one-lung ventilation. Chronic side effects: dyspnea, peripheral neuropathy.
  • Modafinil — promotes wakefulness; approved for narcolepsy, OSA, and shiftwork sleep disorder.
