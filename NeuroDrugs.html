ANTIEPILEPTICS

ANTIEPILEPTIC DRUGS

  • Epilepsy refers to a group of chronic central nervous system disorders characterized by sudden disturbance of sensory, motor, autonomic, or psychic origin
  • Seizures are caused by transient synchronous discharge of groups of neurons in the brain with symptoms depending on the location of discharge and duration
  • Changes in factors such as blood glucose, electrolyte balance, PaO2, pH, stress, or fatigue may facilitate spread of epileptogenic foci into “normal” areas of the brain that previously inhibited seizure foci spread
  • Status epilepticus is continuous seizure activity or more than one seizure occurring in succession without return to consciousness between

ANTIEPILEPTIC DRUGS

  • Seizures are grossly classified based on two factors: loss of consciousness and regions of the brain affected
    • Simple seizure: no loss of consciousness
    • Complex seizure: altered levels of consciousness
    • Partial seizure: originate from limited neurons in a single hemisphere
    • Generalized seizure: activation of neurons in both hemispheres
  • Seizures are treated with antiepileptics initially and combination therapy may be considered if monotherapy fails
    • Goal is to control seizure activity with minimal medication side effects
    • For seizures refractory to medication, surgical intervention may be required (vagal nerve stimulator, laser ablation, neurosurgical resection, etc.)

PHARMACOKINETICS

  • Antiepileptic drugs typically rely upon liver metabolism and hepatic excretion and therefore may need dosage adjustments based on baseline liver and kidney function
  • Several of the drugs are highly protein bound to albumin and can be displaced or affected by various factors (other highly protein bound drugs or hypoalbuminemia)
  • Many of these agents induced metabolizing enzymes therefore lowering the efficacy of other drugs metabolized by the same factors (oral contraceptives and many anesthetics)
  • Phenytoin is unique in that it exhibits non-linear saturation dose kinetics and therefore routine monitoring for plasma concentration is required
    • mcg/mL therapeutic plasma concentration

PHARMACODYNAMICS

  • MOA of these drugs is not completed understood but generally accepted that seizure control is the result of decreased neuronal excitability through alterations in ion currents and/or enhanced inhibition of neurotransmitters
    • Primarily sodium and calcium balance, but sometimes potassium
    • Benzodiazepines (diazepam, lorazepam, and midazolam) provide acute seizure treatment by increasing GABA channel opening
    • Barbiturates increase the duration of GABA channel opening
    • Tiagabine delays the reuptake of GABA, thus prolonging its action
  • Seizure control during pregnancy is important because they can result in significant morbidity and mortality, however, teratogenicity of many of the agents complicate drug management
    • Folate supplementation has been shown to lower the risk of teratogenicity during pregnancy

CARBAMAZEPINE

  • Use: partial and generalized seizure, trigeminal and glossopharyngeal neuralgia
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral only, peak 2-6 hrs, protein binding 80%, half-time 8-24 hours, hepatic metabolism to some active metabolites
  • Notes: Induces its own metabolism and therefore may need a dosage increase 2-4 weeks after starting therapy
    • Also induces metabolism of oral contraceptives, valproic acid, corticosteroids, anticoagulants, and antipsychotics
    • Drugs inhibiting metabolism of carbamazepine: cimetidine, propoxyphene, diltiazem, verapamil, isoniazid, and erythromycin
  • Side effects: most common include sedation, vertigo, diplopia, N/V
    • Rare: aplastic anemia, thrombocytopenia, neutropenia, jaundice, oliguria, hypertension, cardiac dysrhythmia,

ESLICARBAMAZEPINE

  • Use: partial-onset seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral administration, peak 2-4 hrs, 40% protein bound, 90% renal excretion
  • Side effects: most common include N/V, diarrhea, fatigue, dizziness
  • Notes: contraindicated in patients with 2nd and 3rd degree AV block
    • Carbamazepine and phenytoin reduce its plasma concentration through enzyme induction

ETHOSUXIMIDE

  • Use: drug of choice for absence (petit mal) epilepsy without tonic-clonic seizure
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral administration, peak 1-7 hours, 25% excreted unchanged in urine with remainder metabolized by liver, half time 20-60 hours
  • Side effects: N/V, lethargy, dizziness, ataxia, photophobia, hyponatremia, and bone marrow suppression (rare)
  • Notes: Works by decreasing calcium conductance in thalamic nuclei

FELBAMATE

  • Use: intractable epilepsy
  • Pharmacokinetics: mostly excreted unchanged by the kidneys, half life 20 hours, 25% protein binding
  • Major side effects: aplastic anemia, hepatotoxicity
  • Notes: requires monitoring of blood counts and liver function, carbamazepine and phenytoin decrease its plasma concentration, felbamate is a potent inhibitor of P450 enzymes and can slow metabolism of phenytoin, phenobarbital, and valproic acid

GABAPENTIN

  • Use: seizures (limited efficacy), anxiety, panic, and major depression
  • Pharmacokinetics: half life 6 hours, 0% protein binding
  • Side effects: sedation and GI disturbances
  • Notes: analog of GABA but does not bind to GABA receptors, probably works by inhibition of calcium channels

LACOSAMIDE

  • Use: partial onset seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: peak 2-5 hours, almost 100% bioavailability, 20% plasma protein binding
  • Side effects: N/V, diarrhea, headaches, itching, postural hypotension, dysrhythmias
  • Notes: inhibits voltage gated sodium channels and stabilizes hyperexcitable neurons

LAMOTRIGINE

  • Use: partial onset and generalized seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: half life 25 hours, 50% protein binding
  • Side effects: Headache, N/V, diplopia, ataxia, tremor, Steven-Johnson’s syndrome (rare)
  • Notes: stabilizes voltage gated sodium channels, phenytoin, phenobarbital, and carbamazepine decrease its half life by 50%, valproic acid increases it half life to 60 hours

LEVETIRACETAM

  • Use: juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, partial and general convulsive seizures, tonic-clonic seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: no hepatic metabolism and minimal protein binding
  • Side effects: sedation, anxiety, headache
  • Notes: binds to calcium channels and reduces release of neurotransmitters

PERAMPANEL

  • Use: partial onset and generalized tonic-clonic seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: 95% protein bound, elimination half-life 110 hrs, primarily metabolized by liver and excreted 70% in feces and 30% in urine
  • Side effects: Black box warning for suicide or homicidal thoughts, vertigo, slurred speech, irritability, aggression, fatigue, and euphoria
  • Notes: works by selective noncompetitive antagonism of alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors

PHENOBARBITAL

  • Use: long-acting barbiturate effective against all seizure types except non-convulsive generalized seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral absorption is nearly 100% with peak 12-18 hrs, protein binding 50%, 25% elimination by pH dependent renal excretion and 75% hepatic
  • Side effects: sedation, irritability, hyperactivity, depression, confusion, rash, megaloblastic anemia, osteomalacia, nystagmus, ataxia, abnormal collagen deposits manifesting as Dupuytren’s contracture, congenital malformations when given during pregnancy
  • Notes: postsynaptic potentiation of GABA and inhibition of glutamate thus prolonging duration of chloride channel opening, 2nd line drug due to cognitive and behavioral side effects, induces hepatic enzymes thus speeding metabolism of many lipid soluble drugs

PHENYTOIN

  • Use: partial and generalized seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: available oral and IV (therapeutic concentration of 10-20 mcg/mL within 20 min), high therapeutic index with minimal sedation, 90% protein bound, 9-40 hr half life, 98% metabolism by the liver, first order elimination at plasma concentrations <10 mcg/mL, zero order >10 mcg/mL
  • Side effects: nystagmus, ataxia, diplopia, vertigo, peripheral neuropathy, gingival hyperplasia, acne, hirsutism, fetal hydantoin syndrome if given during pregnancy, rash, hyperglycemia and glycosuria from inhibition of insulin secretion, megaloblastic anemia, hepatic toxicity,
  • Notes: regulates sodium and calcium transport thus decreasing neuronal excitability, precipitates in solutions with pH <7.8, IV should be administered at 5mg/min or less, can also be given to suppress cardiac dysrhythmias, induces hepatic enzymes necessary to metabolize many lipid soluble drugs, patients taking this drug will require increased NDNMB doses (increases metabolism of these and patients may also have upregulation of acetylcholine receptors)

STIRIPENTOL

  • Use: Dravet syndrome
  • Pharmacokinetics: rapidly crosses BBB and accumulates in medulla and cerebellum, 99% protein binding, 10-15 hr half life
  • Side effects: weight loss, loss of appetite, somnolence, ataxia, hypotonia, neutropenia, N/V, sleep disorders, hyperexcitability
  • Notes: increases GABA activity, requires WBC monitoring

TIAGABINE

  • Use: adjunct therapy for partial seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: 95% protein binding, 5-8 hrs half-life, hepatic metabolism
  • Side effects: dizziness, aphasia, tremor, mental depression
  • Notes: potent inhibitors of GABA reuptake

TOPIRAMATE

  • Use: partial, generalized tonic-clonic, and absence seizures as well as efficacy for migraines, bulimia, and essential tremor
  • Pharmacokinetics: minimal protein binding and does not affect P450 enzymes
  • Side effects: sedation, dizziness, ataxia, nephrolithiasis
  • Notes: inhibits voltage gated sodium and calcium channels and glutamate, weak inhibitor of carbonic anhydrase

VALPROIC ACID

  • Use: all primary generalized seizures, more effective on convulsive than nonconvulsive seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral administration, peaks in 1-4 hours, 85% protein binding, half time 7-17 hours, hepatic metabolism to active metabolites
  • Side effects: GI upset, weight gain, tremor, thrombocytopenia, hepatotoxicity esp. in children under 2, sedation, ataxia
  • Notes: affects voltage gated sodium channels, can displace phenytoin and diazepam from protein binding sites, inhibits hepatic enzymes necessary for metabolism of phenytoin and phenobarbital

VIGABATRIN

  • Use: refractory complex partial seizures or monotherapy for infantile spasms
  • Pharmacokinetics: not protein bound and undergoes very little metabolism
  • Side effects: permanent visual loss, fatigue, anemia, somnolence
  • Notes: irreversible inhibition of GABA transaminase enzyme thereby increase GABA concentrations, dose should be adjusted for those with renal impairment

ZONISAMIDE

  • Use: adjunct for partial and secondary generalized seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: hepatic metabolism 50% protein binding, 50-70 hr half life
  • Side effects: sedation, dizziness, ataxia, anorexia, mania, nephrolithiasis
  • Notes: calcium inhibition and enhancement of GABA,

BENZODIAZEPINES

DRUGS

  • CLONAZEPAM
  • DIAZEPAM
  • LORAZEPAM
  • MIDAZOLAM
  • CLOBAZAM

ACTIONS

  • Anxiolytic, sedative, muscle-relaxant, and anticonvulsive effects
  • Bind to GABA receptors increasing chloride permeability and leading to cellular hyperpolarization

CLONAZEPAM

  • Use: generally added to other drug therapy or used as first-line for myoclonic seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: rapid absorption with oral administration with peak 2-4 hrs, IV results in rapid CNS effects, 50% protein binding, extensively metabolized, 30-40 hr half life
  • Side effects: sedation, ataxia, behavior disturbances, hyperactivity, irritability, difficultly concentrating, depression, increased secretions
  • Notes: generalized seizures activity may be precipitated if discontinued abruptly

DIAZEPAM

  • Use: mainstay for status epilepticus and local anesthetic induced seizures at 0.1mg/kg IV every 10-15 min until seizure activity suppressed
  • Pharmacokinetics: half-life 27-48 hours, metabolism to active metabolites
  • Side effects: sedation
  • Notes: max dose 30 mg

LORAZEPAM

  • Use: status epilepticus and seizure clusters
  • Pharmacokinetics: oral or IV, 8-25 hr half life, metabolized in the liver without active metabolites
  • Side effects: sedation
  • Notes: longer duration of antiepileptic activity than diazepam because of slow redistribution

CLOBAZAM

  • Use: complex partial, tonic-clonic, and myoclonic seizures
  • Pharmacokinetics: metabolized by liver to active metabolites, half life 16-18 hrs
  • Side effects: sedation (much less effect than other benzodiazepines)
  • Notes: withdrawal if not discontinued gradually

STATUS EPILEPTICUS

  • Medical emergency with prolonged or rapid recurring seizures
  • Goal of treatment: prompt suppression of seizure activity and support the airway & circulation
  • Rule out hypoglycemia (If present give 50 mL D50)
  • Optimize airway and oxygenation (intubation may be needed)
  • Administer antiepileptic/benzodiazepines (propofol/thiopental will temporarily stop seizure
    • benzo not effective: phenytoin, phenobarbital, valproic acid
  • Monitor ABGs as metabolic acidosis is a common sequalae of continued seizure activity (bicarb may be needed)
  • Active cooling may be needed to treat hyperthermia commonly seen

ANTI-PARKINSON DRUGS

ANTI-PARKINSON DRUGS

  • Parkinson’s is a neurodegenerative disorder with loss of dopaminergic fibers normally present in the basal ganglia
  • Dopamine normally inhibits the rate of firing of neurons in control of the extrapyramidal motor system; therefore, depletion results in reduced inhibition and unopposed stimulation by acetylcholine
  • Classic triad of symptoms: skeletal muscle tremor, rigidity, akinesia
  • Facial immobility, pill rolling, diaphragmatic spasms, dementia, and depression are also frequent
  • Treatment aims to increase the concentration of dopamine in the basal ganglia or decrease neuronal effects of acetylcholine
    • Failure of pharmacologic therapy is an indication for deep brain stimulator placement

LEVODOPA

  • Cornerstone of symptomatic treatment for Parkinson’s
  • Precursor to dopamine that crosses BBB and is converted to dopamine by aromatic-L-amino-acid decarboxylase replenishing stores within the basal ganglia
  • Requires frequent dosing due to half-time of 1-3 hours
  • Beneficial effect typically diminishes 5-10 years
  • Abrupt discontinuation can result in quick return of Parkinson’s symptoms and Parkinsonism-Hyperpyrexia syndrome
  • Usually given with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa or benserazide) to maximize entrance into the brain before conversion

LEVODOPA

  • of an orally administered dose is rapidly decarboxylated to dopamine with initial passage through the liver
    • Thus inhibition of peripheral decarboxylase enzyme increases fraction available for cross through BBB
  • Most metabolites are converted to dopamine with some to norepinephrine and epinephrine
  • Further metabolism of dopamine yields 3,4 dihydroxyphenylacetic acid
  • Dietary methionine is necessary for continued action of COMT to metabolize the excessive dopamine
  • Most common side effects within first weeks of therapy are nausea and hypotension with most common long term effects being dyskinesia, mobility changes, confusion, and psychosis

LEVODOPA

  • V occurs in up to 80% of patients and reflects action of dopamine on the CRTZ- usually tends to improve with longer duration of therapy
  • Most effective treatment is domperidone which does not cross the BBB and therefore is unlikely to exacerbate symptoms of Parkinson’s
  • Other dopamine antagonists that cross the BBB such as prochlorperazine, metoclopramide, and promethazine must be avoided in patients with Parkinson’s
  • Cardiac side effects include: alpha and beta stimulation from increased dopamine, epinephrine, and norepinephrine, flushing of the skin, orthostatic hypotension (30% of patients), and cardiac dysrhythmias
    • Orthostatic hypotension may require increased fluid and sodium intake, compression stockings, elevation of the head, fludrocortisone, domperidone, or midodrine.

LEVODOPA

  • Abnormal involuntary movements are also common and develop within 1-4 months of therapy in 50% of patients
    • Tolerance does not develop to this side effect and includes movements such as rocking of the arms, legs, or trunk, irregular respiratory movements, facial tics, and grimacing
    • High protein meals should be avoided because amino acids can interfere with transport of Levodopa to the brain
  • Sudden withdrawal can result in a neuroleptic malignant like disorder characterized by rigidity, pyrexia, autonomic instability, and decreased LOC referred to as parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome
    • Other complications may include: aspiration pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, venous thrombosis, renal failure, and death

LEVODOPA

  • Psychiatric disturbance may reflect progression of the disease or from pharmacologic therapy and usually begins with a nocturnal pattern
  • Drug interactions may occur with the use of Levodopa therapy
    • Antipsychotics such as butyrophenones and phenothiazines can antagonize the effects of dopamine and should not be administered to patients with Parkinson’s
    • Droperidol and Metoclopramide can also interfere with dopamine activity
    • Nonspecific MOAI interfere with inactivation of catecholamines, including dopamine, and can exaggerate the peripheral effects of levodopa
    • Anticholinergics work synergistically with levodopa, however, in large doses may interfere with levodopa absorption within the GI tract
    • Pyridoxine can abolish efficacy of levodopa by enhancing pyridoxine dependent dopa decarboxylase metabolism of levodopa prior to entry within the CNS

PERIPHERAL DECARBOXYLASE INHIBITORS

  • Carbidopa or benserazide are usually administered alongside levodopa to prevent metabolism prior to entry into the CNS
  • These do not cross the BBB and lack activity when administered alone
  • Reduce peripheral side effects of levodopa therapy but do not affect the abnormal involuntary movements or psychiatric disturbances
  • Several combinations of levodopa and a peripheral carboxylase inhibitor are available including Sinemet and Madopar

CATECHOL-O-METHYLTRANSFERASE INHIBITORS

  • COMT is partially responsible for peripheral conversion of levodopa to dopamine and thus its inhibition allows more movement of levodopa through the BBB
  • Tolcapone and Entacapone are both COMT inhibitors within the GI tract which increase plasma concentrations of levodopa or carbidopa by 10-15%
  • Side effects include: induced dyskinesias, N/V, hepatotoxicity (tolcapone), rhabdomyolysis (tolcapone), and orange urine (entacapone)

SYNTHETIC DOPAMINE AGONSITS

  • Drugs include the nonergot alkaloids pramipexole, ropinirole, and rotigotine as well as the tetracyclic ergot alkaloid bromocriptine and these drugs do not require transformation or facilitated transport across the BBB
  • Side effects include: visual and auditory hallucinations, hypotension, dyskinesia, pleuropulmonary fibrosis, erythromelalgia (red, edematous, tender extremites), vertigo, and N/V
  • Abrupt discontinuation can result in parkinsonism-hyperpyrexia syndrome

ANTICHOLINERGICS

  • Trihexyphenidyl and benztropine blunt the effects of the excitatory neurotransmitter acetylcholine and are primarily used as a treatment for tremor
  • They also prevent excessive salivation and have some effects on muscle rigidity and bradykinesia
  • Side effects include hallucinations, confusion, sedation, mydriasis, cycloplegia, adynamic ileus, and urinary retention

AMANTADINE

  • Antiviral used for prophylaxis against influenza A but also improves symptoms in Parkinson’s disease by an unknown mechanism
  • may increase the release of dopamine and slow its reuptake as well as promote some anticholinergic effects and antagonize glutamate & NMDA
  • Has better effect on skeletal muscle rigidity and bradykinesia than anticholinergic drugs
  • Side effects include ankle edema, livedo reticularis of the legs, confusion, psychosis, and potential cardiac failure

MONOAMINE OXIDASE TYPE B ENZYME INHIBITORS

  • Drugs include selegiline, rasagiline, and safinamide
  • MAO-B enzyme is one of the principal catabolic pathways for dopamine within the CNS thus blockade increases the half-time of dopamine
  • Selegiline does not effect metabolism of norepinephrine and thus does not result in life threatening potentiation of its effects
    • Side effects include: confusion, depression, paranoia, and insomnia
  • Rasagiline and Safinamide carry a theoretical risk of serotonin syndrome and avoidance of tyramine is recommended

CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM STIMULANTS

AMPHETAMINE

  • Analeptic prescribed for ADHD, narcolepsy, and obesity that stimulates the release of norepinephrine
    • Central effects include respiratory stimulation, alertness, awakeness, increased muscle strength, concentration, and euphoria
    • Peripheral effects include increased BP, and tachy or reflex bradycardia
    • Acute intoxication increases MAC while chronic use decreases MAC and results in depleted stores of catecholamines
    • Hypotension, hemodynamic instability, and decreased organ perfusion are concerns under anesthesia that require directing acting vasopressors such as epinephrine, phenylephrine, or vasopressin for treatment
  • Methylphenidate is similar to amphetamine and generally prescribed for ADHD with side effects including seizures, HTN, tachycardia, stroke, & MI

DOXAPRAM

  • Analeptic used to augment breathing efforts that results in an increase in tidal volume greater than an increase in frequency
  • Large margin of safety with 20-40 fold difference in dose that stimulates breathing and dose that produces seizures
  • Continuous infusion is needed generally at a dose of 2-3 mg/min to have a sustained effect on ventilation because a single dose only lasts 5-10 minutes
  • Used to help maintain ventilation with COPD patients requiring supplemental oxygen (typically depend on hypoxic drive), to prevent opioid induced ventilatory depression and can also be used to stimulate breathing postoperatively or treat postoperative shivering
  • Side effects include tachycardia, dysrhythmias, increased temp, N/V, and HTN

METHYLXANTHINES

  • Include caffeine, theophylline, and theobromine and have the ability to stimulate the CNS, produce diuresis, increase myocardia contractility, and relax smooth muscle
  • They antagonize the action of adenosine thus facilitating catecholamine release and at high doses can inhibit phosphodiesterase enzymes responsible for breakdown of cAMP
  • Used to treat primary apnea of prematurity by stimulating medullary respiratory centers and increasing sensitivity to CO2
    • Has other uses such as relaxation of bronchial smooth muscle in patients with asthma
  • Single oral dose of 5mg/kg theophylline will produce peak plasma concentration of 10 mcg/mL in adults within 1-2 hours (therapeutic range 10-20 mcg/mL)
  • Side effects include: nervousness, tremors, N/V, dysrhythmias, tachycardia, and seizures (toxicity or rapid IV administration)

METHYLXANTHINES

  • Drugs may enhance (carbamazepine, rifampin) or inhibit (cimetidine, erythromycin) breakdown of theophylline by the liver
  • Ketamine may decrease the seizure threshold of theophylline
  • Theophylline can antagonize the effects of NDNMBs
  • Caffeine can also be used to symptomatically treat post-dural puncture headache at a dose of 75-300 mg oral

OTHER CNS STIMULANTS

  • Almitrine acts on carotid body chemoreceptors to increase minute ventilation, PaO2, and decrease PaCO2
    • May also be used as a measure to prevent hypoxia during one lung ventilation
    • Side effects of chronic therapy include dyspnea and peripheral neuropathy
  • Modafinil promotes wakefulness and approved for excessive daytime somnolence associated with narcolepsy, OSA, and shiftwork sleep disorder

CENTRALLY ACTING MUSCLE RELAXANTS

CENTRALLY ACTING RELAXANTS

  • Primary indication is spasticity accompanying disorders such as stroke, MS, CP, ALS, and CNS injuries
  • Spasticity of skeletal muscle is related to an abnormal increase in resistance to passive movement of a muscle group due to hyperactive proprioceptive or stretch reflexes

BACLOFEN

  • Works on GABAB receptors in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord relieving spasticity by activating G-protein linked presynaptic receptors that hyperpolarize muscle spindle afferent neurons
  • Very effective in flexor spasms and skeletal muscle rigidity with SCI and MS
  • Half-time 3-6 hrs, 80% excreted unchanged in the urine
  • Side effects include sedation, skeletal muscle weakness, confusion, and abrupt withdrawal can result in organ failure, tachycardia, hallucinations, and cardiac arrest while overdose may cause coma, depressed ventilation, and seizures
  • Hypotension is common with chronic oral therapy which may be exacerbated by general anesthesia

OTHER CENTRALLY ACTING AGENTS

  • Diazepam is the most commonly used benzodiazepine and is especially useful for spinal spasticity but use may be limited by sedation
  • Botulinum toxin causes irreversible inhibition of presynaptic acetylcholine release and injection into spastic muscle reduces muscle tone
  • Tizanidine is a short acting alpha 2 adrenergic agonist similar to clonidine reaching peak in 2 hours but only lasting 6 hours
    • Dosage adjustments may be needed for those with renal and hepatic dysfunction and sudden discontinuation can result in hypertension, tachycardia, and hypertonia.
  • Dantrolene exerts antispasmodic effects by decreasing calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum
    • Laboratory investigation for liver dysfunction should be undertaken prior to therapy due to risk of hepatotoxicity
