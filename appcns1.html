Section 1 · General Design
The CNS: Sensory, Motor & Autonomic Axes
The three functional divisions and the neuron structure

Functional Divisions

  • Sensory: Receptors (sensory receptors) bring information from the periphery to the CNS.
  • Motor: Effectors perform functions dictated by nerve signals — includes contraction of skeletal muscle, smooth muscle, and secretion via exocrine/endocrine glands.
  • Autonomic: Operates in parallel to the skeletal motor nerve axis; the automatic, instantaneous response to stimuli.

Neuron Anatomy — Key Terms

  • Soma — main cell body
  • Axon — extends from soma into a peripheral nerve; primary signal conductor
  • Dendrites — branching projections off soma; most fail to transmit action potentials (few voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, excitation threshold too high)
  • Presynaptic membrane — membrane of the signaling neuron (1st neuron)
  • Postsynaptic membrane — membrane of the receiving neuron (2nd neuron)
  • Cerebral cortex — where most memory storage occurs
  • Facilitation — process by which synapses become more capable of transmitting the same sensory signal over time; can eventually fire even without sensory input

Section 2 · Synapses
Chemical vs. Electrical Synapses
Mechanism, transmitters, and where each type is found

Chemical Synapses

  • 1st (presynaptic) neuron secretes a neurotransmitter → acts on receptor proteins in the membrane of the 2nd (postsynaptic) neuron.
  • Can excite, inhibit, or modify sensitivity of the receiving neuron.
  • Synaptic cleft: 200–300 Å wide.
  • Common transmitters: acetylcholine, norepinephrine, epinephrine, histamine, GABA, glycine, serotonin, glutamate.

Electrical Synapses (Gap Junctions)

  • Cytoplasms of adjacent cells directly connected by clusters of ion channels (gap junctions) → free movement of ions between cells.
  • Intercellular gap: 20–40 Å.
  • Found in smooth muscle fibers & cardiac muscle cells.
⭐ High-Yield Chemical synapse = neurotransmitter released into synaptic cleft → ionotropic (ion channel) or metabotropic (second messenger) receptor. Electrical synapse = direct ion flow via gap junctions, no transmitter needed. Smooth muscle and cardiac muscle use electrical synapses for coordinated contraction.

Section 3 · Second Messengers
G Protein & Intracellular Signaling
The cascade from receptor binding to cellular change

What Is a Second Messenger?

A molecule that protrudes into the cell cytoplasm and activates one or more substances inside the postsynaptic neuron.

G Protein System

  • Most common second messenger system in the CNS.
  • Inactive G protein complex (free in cytosol) = GDP + α subunit (activator) + β + γ subunits.

Four Downstream Effects of α-GTP

  • ① Open ion channel in postsynaptic membrane (e.g., K⁺)
  • ② Activate enzyme system in neuron membrane → ↑ cAMP or cGMP → activate metabolic machinery
  • ③ Activate one or more intracellular enzymes → specific cellular chemical activators
  • ④ Activate gene transcription → formation of new proteins & structural changes
⭐ High-Yield Inactivation: GTP bound to α is hydrolyzed → GDP; β and γ reassociate with α → G protein returns to inactive state.

Postsynaptic Response: Excitation vs. Inhibition

ResponseMechanism
Excitation — ↑ membrane potential toward positiveOpening Na⁺ channels; depressed conductance of Cl⁻/K⁺; changes in internal metabolism ↑ excitatory receptors
Inhibition — ↑ negativity inside neuronOpening Cl⁻ channels; ↑ K⁺ conductance out; ↑ inhibitory receptors; can occur pre- or postsynaptically

Summation & Convergence

  • Spatial Summation — signal strength increases by recruiting progressively more fibers; stronger signals spread to more fibers.
  • Temporal Summation — increasing the frequency of nerve impulses in each fiber.
  • Convergence — signals from multiple inputs uniting to excite a single neuron; allows summation of information from different sources.
⭐ CRNA Connection Anesthetics work by decreasing neuronal excitability. Hypoxia & acidosis also depress the CNS — relevant when interpreting altered mental status perioperatively.

Neurotransmitters: Small-Molecule & Neuropeptide Classes
Where each is secreted and its primary role

Small-Molecule Transmitters (Rapidly Acting)

Synthesized in the cytosol of the presynaptic terminal; absorbed via active transport into vesicles; recycled and reused.

Neuropeptides (Large-Molecule, Slower Acting)

  • Synthesized by ribosomes in the neuronal cell body as large protein molecules.
  • Vesicle is autolyzed and NOT reused (unlike small molecule transmitters).
  • Often more potent and cause prolonged actions.
  • Examples: growth hormone, substance P, vasopressin, insulin, glucagon, bradykinin.
⭐ High-Yield GABA = primary CNS inhibitory transmitter. Glutamate = primary CNS excitatory transmitter. Dopamine is secreted in the substantia nigra — loss here causes Parkinson's disease. Substance P and glutamate are the pain neurotransmitters (slow and fast, respectively).

Types of Sensory Receptors & Adaptation
Five receptor classes and tonic vs. phasic adaptation

NociceptorsPhysical or chemical damage (pain receptors — free nerve endings)
Electromagnetic ReceptorsLight on the retina

Adaptation

  • Sensory receptors adapt partially or completely to a constant stimulus over time.
  • Continuous stimulus → receptor fires rapidly at first, then progressively slower, until few or no APs fire.
  • Tonic receptors — slow-adapting; include vestibular macula receptors, pain receptors, baroreceptors, chemoreceptors of carotid & aortic bodies.
⭐ High-Yield Pain receptors (nociceptors = free nerve endings) adapt very poorly — hyperalgesia is a demonstration of this. Contrast with rapidly-adapting touch receptors that stop firing quickly.

Somatosensory Cortex

  • Central sulcus (fissure of Rolando) — extends horizontally; sensory signals terminate posterior to it; motor cortex is anterior.
  • Somatosensory Area I — posterior to central fissure (postcentral gyrus, Brodmann's areas 3, 1, & 2); high degree of localization; does NOT include pain & temperature.
  • Somatosensory Area II — poor degree of localization.
  • Brodmann's Areas — 52 distinct cortical areas based on histological structural differences.

Dermatomes

Each spinal nerve innervates a segmental skin field. Despite appearing distinct, there is overlap between adjacent dermatomes.

⭐ High-Yield Dorsal column crosses at the MEDULLA. Anterolateral (spinothalamic) crosses at SPINAL CORD LEVEL. This is the anatomical basis for Brown-Séquard syndrome: ipsilateral motor and dorsal column loss, contralateral spinothalamic loss (pain/temp).

Section 10 · Pain
Pain Types, Causes, Transmission & Analgesia System
Aδ vs. C fiber pain, the endogenous analgesic pathway, and referred pain

Types of Pain

TypeOnsetQualityStimulusFiber
Fast pain<0.1 secSharp, pricking, acute, electricMechanical & thermalAδ fibers
Slow pain>1 sec, buildsBurning, aching, throbbing, chronicTissue destruction (mechanical, thermal, chemical)C fibers

Causes of Pain

  • Mechanical — tissue disruption
  • Thermal — temperature extremes
  • Chemical — bradykinin, serotonin, histamine, K⁺ ions, acids, acetylcholine, proteolytic enzymes excite pain receptors; prostaglandins & substance P enhance sensitivity
  • Tissue ischemia — lactic acid accumulation + bradykinin + proteolytic enzymes
  • Muscle spasm — direct stimulation of mechanoreceptors + compression of blood vessels

Pain Transmission Pathways

Key Term Fast pain (Aδ → Neospinothalamic pathway): Lamina I of dorsal horn → 2nd-order neuron → anterolateral column → reticular areas of brain stem (few) + thalamus (most) → somatosensory cortex. Neurotransmitter: Glutamate.

Slow pain (C → Paleospinothalamic pathway): Laminae II & III (substantia gelatinosa) → 2nd-order neuron (short) → lamina V → anterolateral pathway → brain stem (reticular nuclei, tectal area, periaqueductal gray). Neurotransmitters: Glutamate + Substance P.

→

Neurotransmitters: Enkephalin, endorphins, serotonin. Morphine-like drugs inactivate these pathways → suppress pain signals from peripheral nerves.

Referred Pain

  • Definition: pain felt in a body part remote from the tissue causing it.
  • Mechanism: visceral pain fibers synapse on the SAME 2nd-order neurons that receive skin signals — brain misattributes the source.
  • Localized to the dermatomal segment from which the visceral organ originated embryologically (not where it now lies). Example: heart originated in neck/upper thorax → pain referred to C3–T5.
  • Two pathways: Visceral (via autonomic nerve bundles) vs. Parietal (direct spinal nerves, more localized).
⭐ High-Yield Stimulation of Aβ sensory fibers can depress pain signal transmission from the same area — basis for TENS and rubbing near painful skin. The periaqueductal gray is the key site for opioid analgesia.

Section 11 · Pain Abnormalities
Herpes Zoster, Trigeminal Neuralgia, Brown-Séquard, CRPS & Headache
Perioperatively relevant pain syndromes

Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

Infects the dorsal root ganglion → severe pain in the dermatomal segment supplied by that ganglion.

Trigeminal Neuralgia (Tic Douloureux)

  • Also called glossopharyngeal neuralgia.
  • Stabbing pain over one side of the face in the sensory distribution of cranial nerve V or IX.
  • Usually triggered by a mechanoreceptive stimulus (light touch, chewing, cold air).

Brown-Séquard Syndrome (Hemisection of spinal cord)

Headache

  • Brain tissue itself is almost totally insensitive to pain.
  • Pain-sensitive areas: venous sinuses, tentorium, dura at base of brain, meningeal blood vessels, middle meningeal artery.
  • Intracranial hypotension (CSF leak, dural tear) → distorts dural structures → pain relieved by horizontal position → treatment: supportive, epidural blood patch, or surgery.

Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)

  • May follow injury or surgery, most frequently in a limb.
  • Symptoms: pain, swelling, ↓ hair growth, skin changes, bone demineralization.
  • Budapest Criteria for diagnosis: continued/disproportionate pain + ≥1 symptom in ≥3 of 4 categories (sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor, motor/trophic).
  • Management: prevention with vitamin C supplementation after injury, multimodal pain control, physical therapy.

Section 12 · Vision
Eye Anatomy, Autonomic Effects, IOP, Retina & Eye Movement
Perioperative ophthalmic complications and pupil control

Corneal Abrasion

  • Most common perioperative ophthalmic complication.
  • Signs: pain, foreign body sensation, photophobia, excessive tearing, conjunctival erythema.
  • Etiology: corneal drying, reduced tear production, direct trauma.
  • Diagnosis: fluorescein stain + cobalt blue penlight.
  • Prevention: tape eyelids closed. Treatment: antibiotic drops, topical NSAIDs, eye patch — often self-limiting.

Fluid System of the Eye

  • Aqueous humor — in front of lens; freely flowing; continually formed & reabsorbed → regulates IOP.
  • Vitreous humor — between lens and retina; gelatinous, held by fine fibrillar network.
  • Canal of Schlemm — thin-walled vein circling the eye → empties into extraocular veins; normally contains aqueous humor (not blood).

Autonomic Effects on the Eye

DivisionEffect
ParasympatheticCiliary muscle contraction (focus near); Pupil constriction (miosis)
SympatheticCiliary muscle relaxation (focus far); Pupil dilation (mydriasis); Eyelid elevation

Intraocular Pressure (IOP)

  • Normal: ~15 mmHg.
  • Glaucoma — pathologically high IOP compresses optic nerve axons at optic disc → common cause of blindness.
  • Mechanism: ↑ resistance to aqueous outflow through trabecular spaces in Canal of Schlemm.
  • Treatment: eye drops (↓ secretion or ↑ reabsorption of aqueous humor); operative techniques to open trabecular spaces.

Retina

  • Light-sensitive layer at back of eye; converts light → electrical signals via photoreceptors (rods & cones) → optic nerve → brain.
  • Blood supply: central retinal artery (internal layers); choroid (outer layers — highly vascular tissue between retina & sclera).
  • Posterior segment of optic nerve has significantly LESS baseline blood flow than anterior segment — makes it more vulnerable to ischemia.
⚠ Perioperative Visual Complications Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (ION): Suspect with visual loss in first week post-op.
— Anterior ION: sudden, painless monocular deficit; arteritic (ciliary artery inflammation) or nonarteritic (↓ O₂ delivery to optic disc from hypotension/anemia); minimal recovery.
— Posterior ION: more common perioperatively; ↓ O₂ to posterior optic nerve + impaired venous outflow; risk: male sex, obesity, Wilson frame, long procedure, ↑ blood loss.
Cortical Blindness: May follow profound hypotension or circulatory arrest.
Retinal Artery Occlusion: Painless monocular blindness; emboli from carotid atherosclerotic plaque; visual fields may partially improve.

Eye Muscles & Movement

  • 3 pairs: medial/lateral recti (side to side), superior/inferior recti (up/down), superior/inferior obliques (rotation).
  • Innervated by CN III, IV, VI.
  • Strabismus — squint/cross-eye; lack of fusion in horizontal, vertical, or rotational coordinates.

Pupillary Diameter & Horner's Syndrome

  • Light reflex: afferent = CN II (optic); efferent = CN III (oculomotor).
  • Diseases blocking pupillary reflexes: syphilis, alcoholism, encephalitis.
⭐ Horner's Syndrome Results from interruption of sympathetic nerves to the eye. Classic triad: miosis (pupil constriction), ptosis (eyelid droop), anhidrosis (no sweating) on the affected side. Also: flushing/redness on affected side.

Section 13 · Hearing & Taste
Ear Anatomy & Neuronal Transmission of Taste
Structural overview of outer/middle/inner ear and taste pathway

Ear Anatomy

  • Outer ear: External acoustic meatus → tympanic membrane.
  • Middle ear: Ossicles — malleus, incus, stapes; stapedius & tensor tympani muscles; oval & round windows; Eustachian tube.
  • Inner ear: Cochlea (hearing — scala tympani, scala vestibuli); vestibular nerve; cochlear nerve.

Taste Pathway

  • Taste buds in papillae of the tongue.
  • Anterior 2/3 tongue: cranial nerve VII (chorda tympani branch).
  • Posterior 1/3 tongue: cranial nerve IX (glossopharyngeal).
  • Signals transmitted centrally via CN VII and IX to the brain stem.

Section 14 · Alzheimer & Parkinson
Alzheimer Disease & Parkinson Disease
Pathology, mechanism, and treatment strategy

Alzheimer Disease

  • Chronic, neurodegenerative disease; most common cause of dementia.
  • Hallmark pathology: diffuse amyloid beta-rich plaques and neurofibrillary tangles.
  • Patients often confused and uncooperative — important anesthetic consideration for consent and postop monitoring.

Parkinson Disease

  • Neurodegenerative disorder with characteristic loss of dopaminergic fibers in the basal ganglia.
  • Dopamine normally inhibits firing rate of extrapyramidal motor neurons. Loss → ↓ inhibition → unopposed acetylcholine stimulation.
  • Classic triad: skeletal muscle tremor, rigidity, akinesia.
⭐ Treatment Strategy — Two Approaches 1. Increase dopamine concentration in basal ganglia (levodopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors).
2. Decrease neuronal effects of acetylcholine (anticholinergics).
Failure of pharmacologic therapy → deep brain stimulator (DBS).

Section 16 · Periop Neurocognitive
Postoperative Delirium & Postop Cognitive Dysfunction (POCD)
Risk factors, diagnosis, and management

Postoperative Delirium (POD)

  • Acute disruption of perception, thinking, memory, psychomotor behavior, sleep-wake cycle, consciousness, attention.
  • Can be hypoactive or hyperactive.
  • Typically manifests acutely; can last days to weeks.
⭐ POD Risk Factors — Know These Strongest predisposing factor: preexisting cognitive impairment / dementia.
Others: age >65, male gender, EEG suppression during anesthesia, ETOH abuse, depression, longer duration of anesthesia, poor functional status, abnormal electrolytes, Parkinson's disease, CV disease, dehydration, metabolic disease, anticholinergics used intraop, ICU admission, inadequate pain control, ASA ≥3, low serum albumin, aortic or orthopedic surgery.

Diagnosing Delirium — Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)

Requires features (1) and (2) AND either (3) or (4):

  • (1) Acute onset & fluctuating course — evidence of acute change in mental status
  • (2) Inattention — difficulty focusing
  • (3) Disorganized thinking — incoherent
  • (4) Altered level of consciousness — any answer other than "alert"

Treatment of POD

  • Target reversible risk factors.
  • Limit sedatives; avoid drugs with atropine (anticholinergic) properties.
  • Judicious treatment of pain; careful use of antipsychotics.
  • Nonpharmacologic interventions.

Postop Cognitive Dysfunction (POCD)

  • Array of cognitive impairments: memory deficit, ↓ concentration, impaired comprehension, delayed psychomotor speed.
  • Onset subtle; weeks to months after surgery (vs. acute for POD).
  • No universally accepted diagnostic criteria — requires neurocognitive testing pre & post-op.
  • Predominant risk factor: age >65. Others: lower education, longer anesthesia duration, preop depression/cognitive decline, postop infection.
  • Important: Surgery and anesthesia are unlikely to cause persistent cognitive decline or dementia — relationship is unclear.

Section 17 · Depression & GBS
Depression & Guillain-Barré Syndrome
Neurotransmitter basis of depression and GBS anesthetic implications

Depression

  • Evidence suggests diminished norepinephrine and/or serotonin responsible for symptoms.
  • Treatment: drugs that increase NE and serotonin.
  • Drug classes: SSRIs (Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors), Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs).

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (Acute Idiopathic Polyneuritis)

  • Rare, autoimmune disorder causing multifocal inflammatory demyelination of peripheral nerves.
  • Mechanism: antibody & cell-mediated immunologic reaction.

GBS Clinical Implications

DomainManifestation
Ventilation / AirwayIntercostal weakness; pharyngeal muscle weakness → dysphagia; may require mechanical ventilation
MuscleSudden onset skeletal muscle weakness/paralysis beginning in legs → spreads cephalad (arms, trunk, face); denervation → upregulation of ACh receptors
SensoryParesthesia and pain
AutonomicBP fluctuations, sudden diaphoresis, peripheral vasoconstriction, resting tachycardia, cardiac conduction abnormalities
Thromboembolism↑ risk due to immobility
⚠ GBS — Critical Anesthetic Concern Denervation → upregulation of ACh receptors across the entire muscle membrane → use of succinylcholine can cause life-threatening hyperkalemia. Avoid succinylcholine in GBS patients.

GBS Diagnosis

Features required: progressive bilateral weakness in arms & legs + areflexia.

Supporting: symptom progression 2–4 weeks, symmetry, mild sensory signs, cranial nerve involvement, spontaneous recovery starting 2–4 weeks after progression halts, ANS dysfunction, elevated CSF protein with cell count <10/mm³, ↓ nerve conduction velocity.

Makes diagnosis unlikely: definite sensory level, marked persistent asymmetry, persistent bowel/bladder dysfunction, >50 WBCs/mm³ in CSF.

Treatment

  • Symptomatic management (supportive care).
  • Mechanical ventilation as necessary.
  • DVT prophylaxis.
