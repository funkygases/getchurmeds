PT.1 · 1 Vessel Layers & Endothelial Cell Function
▾
High Yield — Vessel Layers (Memorize All Three)
LayerPositionCompositionKey Function
Tunica IntimaInnermostEndothelial cells + elastic & connective tissue; sub-endothelium beneathSeparates blood from highly thrombogenic material below
Tunica MediaMiddleSmooth muscle, collagen, elastic tissueVERY THROMBOGENIC — collagen & fibronectin anchor platelets to injury sites. Under control of vasoactive mediators
Tunica AdventitiaOutermostPrimarily connective & elastic tissueStructural support; under control of vasoactive mediators from endothelial cells
CapillariesExceptionIntima ONLY — no elastic tissueVery thin → allows diffusion of gas & substances (gas exchange)
Instructor Note

The media contains collagen and fibronectin which anchor platelets to sites of injury — this is how the clotting cascade begins. Capillaries only have the intima — this is why they facilitate gas exchange.

Endothelial Cell Products

Endothelial cells separate fluid from thrombogenic subendothelial material and synthesize/secrete a balance of procoagulants, anticoagulants, and fibrinolytics:

  • von Willebrand factor (vWF) — adheres platelets to subendothelial layer
  • Tissue factor (III) — activates the clotting cascade
  • Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) — aids in suppression of coagulation
  • TPA & urokinase — activate plasminogen to initiate fibrinolysis

The smoothness of the endothelial lining + the glycocalyx layer (gel-like glycoprotein coat) both PREVENT clotting in the normal vascular system.

Mediators of Vessel Tone (After Injury)

  • Upon injury, the vessel wall contracts via myogenic spasm, platelet autocoids, and autonomic reflexes
  • Thromboxane A₂ & ADP — released from platelets → VASOCONSTRICTION at injury site
  • Nitric oxide & Prostacyclin — released from adjacent healthy endothelial cells → VASODILATION (diverts blood flow away from injury to healthy tissues)
Instructor Note

NO pathway: L-arginine → (NOS) → NO → diffuses into smooth muscle cell → activates soluble guanylate cyclase → GTP → cGMP → vasodilation. This pathway has pharmacological implications — it relates to how nitroglycerin works.

Three stages of primary hemostasis then follow: Adhesion → Activation → Aggregation

PT.1 · 2 Primary Hemostasis — Platelet Plug Formation
▾
High Yield — Platelet Properties
  • Formed from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow
  • 1–4 micrometers in diameter — smaller than RBCs, WBCs, electrolytes, proteins → strategically pushed to vessel wall edges near endothelium (exactly where needed)
  • Half-life: 8–12 days; removed primarily by the spleen
  • Normal count: 150,000–350,000/mm³
  • Largely inactive until stimulated by vessel trauma
  • Do NOT contain a nucleus and cannot reproduce — similar to whole cells but anucleate
  • Contain actin & myosin (contraction), can make ATP
  • Contain alpha & dense granules storing proteins and non-proteins
  • Granule contents: fibrinogen, serotonin, ADP, TXA₂, histamine, epinephrine, phospholipids, HMWK, pre-kallikrein
  • ~7,100 platelets used daily to patch microscopic vascular injuries
  • Adequate numbers AND function both required — normal count ≠ normal function

3 Steps of Platelet Plug Formation

Step 1
Adhesion
vWF → GP1B receptor; platelets change shape: disc → spiky oval
Step 2
Activation
vWF outside-in signaling → phospholipase C + Ca²⁺ → granule release (TXA₂, ADP, thrombin)
Step 3
Aggregation
GP2B/3A receptors link platelets via fibrin/fibrinogen → white clot forms
Result
Red Clot
Fibrin mesh strengthens white clot → secondary (red) clot
High Yield — Step Details

Step 1 — Adhesion

  • vWF emerges from endothelial cells and attaches to glycoprotein 1B (GP1B) receptors on platelet surface
  • vWF also attracts more platelets and increases stickiness
  • Platelets change shape: disc → oval with irregular spiky pseudopod protrusions

Step 2 — Activation

  • vWF binding initiates outside-in signaling → activates phospholipase C
  • Tissue factor, thrombin, ADP, TXA₂ all stimulate GP receptors → more phospholipase C activation
  • Phospholipase C + calcium → change in platelet shape
  • Contractile proteins release contents of alpha & dense granules (thrombin, TXA₂, procoagulant mediators)
  • Granule release initiates the coagulation cascade and fibrin formation

Step 3 — Aggregation

  • GP2B/3A receptors (fibrinogen fiber receptors) change shape and link platelets together with fibrin/fibrinogen
  • Activated phospholipase C → inside-out signaling → stimulates GP2B/3A shape change
  • Initial clump = white clot (primary/initial clot)
  • ADP & TXA₂ from granules act on nearby platelets → stimulate adherence
  • After full coagulation: fibrinogen & fibrin strengthen clot → red clot / secondary clot
Instructor Note

Normal uninjured endothelium nearby releases prostacyclin (PGI₂) + NO to INHIBIT platelet aggregation — prevents the clot from growing too far from the site of injury. This is a critical limiting mechanism.

PT.1 · 3 Clotting Factors (I–XIII)
▾
High Yield — Know Number AND Name (Exam uses either!)
  • Active form = factor number + "a" (e.g., Factor IIa = thrombin)
  • Factor IV (Calcium) is required in ALMOST ALL coagulation events
  • No Factor VI — originally listed, found to be a duplicate of Factor V, removed
  • Vitamin K-dependent factors: II, VII, IX, X — mnemonic: "1972"
  • Most clotting factors are zymogens (inactive enzyme precursors) until activated
🧠 Mnemonic — Liver vs. Non-Liver

"Three and four, the liver does not store" — Factors III (tissue factor, from tissue cells) and IV (calcium, from plasma) are NOT made in the liver. All others are liver-made.

PT.1 · 4 Coagulation Cascade — Extrinsic, Intrinsic & Common Pathways
▾
Coagulation Cascade — Full Visual
High Yield — Extrinsic Pathway
  • Activated by: damage outside the vessel (traumatized tissue contacts blood)
  • Tissue factor (III) released from endothelial cells + Factor VII (Proconvertin) + Calcium (IV) → activate Factor X
  • Simultaneously: TFPI released → immediately begins to INHIBIT tissue factor (limits clot size)
  • Speed: ~30 seconds — FASTER (fewer steps)
Mnemonic

"For 37 cents you can purchase the extrinsic pathway" = Factors 3 & 7

High Yield — Intrinsic Pathway
  • Activated by: exposure of blood to collagen within traumatized vessels (damage inside the vessel)
  • Initiated by: platelet phospholipids (platelet factor 3), HMWK, pre-kallikrein (all from platelets)
  • Hageman Factor (XII) activated → XI → IX (Christmas factor) → VIII (antihemophilic)
  • VIII + IX + calcium + platelet phospholipids → activate Factor X
  • Speed: few minutes (more steps = SLOWER)
Mnemonic

"$12 to $11.98" = Factors 12, 11, 9, 8

High Yield — Common Pathway
  • Both pathways converge at Factor X activation
  • Xa + Va + Calcium (IV) → form Prothrombin Activator = RATE-LIMITING STEP
  • Prothrombin activator cleaves II (Prothrombin) → IIa (Thrombin)
  • Thrombin activates I (Fibrinogen) → Ia (Fibrin)
  • Factor XIII (activated by thrombin) → covalent bonding + crosslinking of fibrin fibers → STABLE CLOT
  • Early fibrin: held by weak hydrogen bonds. After few minutes: covalent bonds → much stronger
  • Clot retraction: contractile proteins + calcium within platelets extrude serum, pull vessel walls together
  • Failure of clot retraction = indicator of low platelet levels
Mnemonic

"5 and 10 store for $1 or $2 on the 13th" = Factors 5, 10, 1, 2, 13

Instructor Note

Pathways once believed to work independently — now understood to work in conjunction / simultaneously. Extrinsic = damage outside vessel; Intrinsic = damage inside vessel. Suggestion: write factor numbers AND names beside cascade diagrams while studying. Add calcium, phospholipids, and prothrombin activator to your diagrams.

PT.1 · 5 Thrombin & Fibrinolysis
▾
High Yield — Thrombin Roles (Factor IIa)

Thrombin is a POSITIVE FEEDBACK mechanism:

  • Proteolytic actions on many clotting factors — assists activation of: Factors I, V, VIII, IX, X, XI, XII, and XIII
  • Recruits more platelets to site of injury
  • Aids in acceleration of clot retraction
  • 85–90% becomes bound within fibrin fibers — PREVENTS excess clot formation
  • Binds to thrombomodulin (on endothelial membrane) → begins ANTICOAGULATION processes
  • Activates Protein C → inactivates Factors V & VIII
  • Protein S increases the ability of Protein C
  • Proteins C & S = ANTICOAGULANTS (Nagelhout textbook incorrectly lists as procoagulants)

Fibrinolysis

Once the vessel is sealed, the fibrinolytic system takes over to break down the fibrin clot over days.

High Yield — Fibrinolysis Pathway
  • Major pathway: TPA + urokinase (from endothelial cells, released days after clot forms) → convert plasminogen → plasmin → degrades fibrin
  • Minor pathway: Factors XIa, XIIa, and kallikrein
  • Plasmin also inactivates: Factors I, II, V, VIII, XIII
  • Heparin (from basophils & mast cells) alone = little anticoagulant ability. Binding to Antithrombin III (AT-III) → increases AT-III action 1000×
  • AT-III inhibits: Factors IX, X, XI, XII + thrombin
  • Once clot digested: alpha-antiplasmin + tPA activator inhibitor stop fibrinolysis
Promoters of fibrinolysis: TPA, urokinase → plasminogen → plasmin → fibrin degradation products
Inhibitors of fibrinolysis: PAI-1, PAI-2, alpha-2 antiplasmin, alpha-2 macroglobulin, TAFI
Instructor Note

Write down all enzymes and their functions to avoid confusion between procoagulants, anticoagulants, fibrinolytic activators, and fibrinolytic inhibitors. Protein C & S: anticoagulants. Protein C inhibits V & VIII. Protein S augments Protein C.
