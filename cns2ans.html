CNS Structures & Spinal Anatomy

Posterior Neurons (Dorsal)

  • Sensory signals enter the cord almost entirely through the dorsal (sensory) roots
  • After entering, signal travels to 2 places:
    • Gray matter of the cord — Elicits local segmental cord reflexes & other local effects
    • Higher levels

Anterior Neurons

  • Innervate the skeletal muscle fibers
  • 2 Types of Neurons:
    • Alpha Motor Neuron — Gives rise to Type A alpha motor nerve fibers; stimulate motor units
    • Gamma Motor Neuron — Transmit via Type A gamma motor nerve fibers; constitute the muscle spindle (helps control basic muscle tone)
  • Brain areas involved: Cerebellum, basal ganglia, cerebral cortex

Interneurons

  • Present in all areas of the cord gray matter
  • Small, highly excitable (capable of spontaneous activity)
  • These interconnections are responsible for most of the integrative functions of the spinal cord

Brain Stem

  • Consists of medulla, pons, & mesencephalon (midbrain)
  • Can be considered an extension of the spinal cord (contains motor & sensory nuclei for face & head)
  • Special functions:
    • Control of respiration
    • Control of cardiovascular system
    • Partial control of gastrointestinal function
    • Movements: Stereotyped, equilibrium, eyes

The layers you'll pass through with your needle →

Layers — Outer to Inner (Skin → CSF)

  • Skin / SQ tissue
  • Supraspinous Ligament — Connects tips of vertebral spines
  • Interspinous Ligament — Connects the spaces between the vertebral spines
  • Ligamentum Flavum — Strong ligament along the posterior aspect of the vertebral canal; distinct feel to go through → enter epidural space
  • Epidural Space — Space containing fat & veins between flavum & dura; LOR (Loss of Resistance); WHERE ANESTHESIA IS INJECTED; smooth, no resistance; needs large needle; NO CSF here
  • Dura Mater — Outermost layer of meninges
  • Subdural Space — Under normal conditions not a real space — trauma can create it
  • Arachnoid Mater — Middle layer of meninges (between dura & pia)
  • Subarachnoid Space — Filled with CSF — Spinal anesthetic goes here
  • Pia Mater — Inner layer, closest to brain/cord

Vertebral Anatomy

  • Spinous process, Transverse process
  • Lamina — Region of bone between spinous & transverse process
  • Pedicle — Segment of bone connecting transverse processes with the vertebral body
  • Cord extends from medulla oblongata to lumbar region (L1–L2)
  • Note: Conus medullaris, cauda equina
  • Vertebral formula: 7 cervical / 12 thoracic / 5 lumbar
⭐ High Yield — Spine Landmarks
  • C7 — Bony knob at base of neck
  • T6 — Lowest point when patient lies flat
  • T7–T8 — Lower limits of scapulae
  • L2 — Terminal point of 12th ribs; end of spinal cord
  • L3 — High point when patient lies flat
  • L4 — Line across iliac crests
  • S2 — Posterior iliac spines; lowest point when patient lies flat
  • Cervical & lumbar curves → convex toward abdomen; Sacral & thoracic → convex toward back
⭐ High Yield — Injection Volumes & Wet Tap
  • Spinal (Subarachnoid): ≤ 2 mL
  • Epidural: 1–2 mL / level of coverage (e.g., 2–4 levels = 2–8 mL)
  • Wet Tap: Accidental dural puncture → Post-Dural Puncture Headache (PDPH) → treat with epidural blood patch
  • Caudal Block: Palpate sacral horn, insert needle to interact with epidural space
📝 Exam Tip

Know the anatomical terms for the vertebrae AND what you do at each step advancing the needle: supraspinous → interspinous → flavum → LOR = epidural; through dura/arachnoid = subarachnoid/spinal.

Spinal Cord Injury & Motor Pathways

Acute Spinal Cord Injury

  • Cervical spine vulnerable to hyperextension
  • If any doubt, all acute cervical spine injuries treated as unstable until proven otherwise
⭐ High Yield — Acute SCI Pearls
  • "C3, 4, & 5 keep the diaphragm alive" — Injury above C5 may result in APNEA
  • Kyphosis = Thoracic; Lordosis = Lumbar
  • Spinal Shock: Flaccid muscle paralysis with loss of sensation BELOW the level of injury (neurogenic shock = autonomic manifestations from CNS injury)

Chronic SCI & Autonomic Hyperreflexia

  • After acute injury, spinal cord reflexes gradually return → overactivity of SNS & involuntary muscle spasms
  • Sequelae: impaired alveolar ventilation, pulmonary infections, genitourinary infections, renal dysfunction, altered thermoregulation, osteoporosis, decubitus ulcers, & autonomic hyperreflexia

Autonomic Hyperreflexia

  • Associated with return of spinal cord reflexes
  • Stimulation below injury level → afferent impulses → ↑ sympathetic activity along splanchnic outflow (NOT inhibited from above)
  • Result: Vasoconstriction below injury, reflex bradycardia, cutaneous vasodilation above injury
  • Cause: Cutaneous or visceral stimulation below the level of injury
⭐ High Yield — Autonomic Hyperreflexia Incidence
  • 85% of patients with lesions above T6 will exhibit autonomic hyperreflexia
  • Hypotension unlikely with lesions below T10
  • Incidence decreases the further out from injury
  • MORE ANESTHESIA NEEDED if patient enters autonomic hyperreflexia

Congenital Anomalies & Degenerative Diseases

  • Spina Bifida: Occulta (NO true pouch), Meningocele (WITH a pouch), Myelomeningocele (cord exposed in the pouch)
  • Tethered Spinal Cord Syndrome — Abnormal attachments → cord stretching; conus often below L2
  • Syringomyelia — Cystic cavitation of spinal cord
  • Friedreich Ataxia — Autosomal recessive; degeneration of spinocerebellar & pyramidal tracts; cardiomyopathy in 10–50%

Flexor Reflex

  • Cutaneous sensory stimulation (usually nociceptive) → flexor muscles contract → limb withdrawal
  • Pathway: NOT directly to anterior motor neurons → first into spinal cord interneurons → then secondarily to motor neurons

Corticospinal Tract (Pyramidal Tract)

  • Primary output pathway from motor cortex
Motor cortex→ Posterior limb of internal capsule (between caudate nucleus & putamen of basal ganglia)→ Brain stem (pyramids of medulla)→ Cross in lower medulla→ Lateral corticospinal tracts→ Interneurons in the intermediate regions of cord gray matter

A few fibers pass ipsilaterally in the ventral corticospinal tracts (don't cross).

Cerebellum

  • Functions in association with other systems of motor control (accessory motor system)
  • Essential during rapid muscular movement (running, typing, piano, talking)
  • Helps sequence & monitor motor activities; makes corrective adjustments

Clinical Abnormalities:

  • Dysmetria — Inability to control distance, speed, & range of movements
  • Ataxia — Uncoordinated movements that result from dysmetria
  • Dysarthria — Difficulty speaking
  • Nystagmus — Tremor of the eyeballs (usually occurs when one attempts to fixate the eyes on a scene to one side of the head)

Basal Ganglia

  • Principal role: functions with corticospinal system to control complex motor patterns (writing, cutting, hammering, ball sports, eye movement)
  • Consists of: caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, substantia nigra, subthalamic nucleus

Clinical Abnormalities & Diseases:

  • Athetosis — Continuous writhing movements of hand, arm, neck, or face
  • Hemiballismus — Flailing movements of limbs
  • Akinesia — Inability to initiate movements
  • Parkinson's Disease — Widespread degeneration of the substantia nigra → muscle rigidity, involuntary tremor, akinesia, postural instability, dysphagia
  • Huntington's Disease — Autosomal dominant; flicking → severe distortional movements; severe dementia; loss of cell bodies of the GABA-secreting neurons in the caudate nucleus & loss of acetylcholine in many parts of the brain
  • ALS (Lou Gehrig's) — Degenerative disease of lower motor neuron (anterior horn) & upper motor neuron/corticospinal tracts

Brain Activity, Cerebral Blood Flow & Cranial Nerves

Limbic System

  • Entire neuronal circuitry that controls emotional behavior & motivational drives
  • Hypothalamus — Central element; role in: cardiovascular regulation, temperature, osmolality, uterine contractility, milk ejection, GI/feeding regulation, endocrine hormone secretion

States of Brain Activity

  • Consciousness — Continuing stream of awareness of surroundings or sequential thoughts; may or may not produce amnesia
  • Anterograde Amnesia — Inability to form NEW memories after an event (most anesthesia meds)
  • Retrograde Amnesia — Loss of access to events/information learned PRIOR to an event

Sleep

  • Unconsciousness from which a person CAN be aroused by sensory or other stimuli
  • Current theory: sleep is caused by an active inhibitory process
⭐ High Yield — Sleep Types
  • REM Sleep: Eyes undergo rapid movements (5–30 minutes); ~25% of sleep time in young adults; episode recurs ~every 90 min; 'active' form associated with dreaming & active bodily muscle movements; difficult to arouse; drugs that mimic ACH increase occurrence
  • Slow-wave (Non-REM): Deep, restful; decreases in peripheral vascular tone; dreams can occur but usually not remembered
  • Mnemonic: "Beta Riding Delta" = Sleep states from awake to deep

Orexin Neurons

  • Orexin/hypocretin — produced by hypothalamic neurons, provide excitatory input
  • Most active during waking; stop firing during slow wave & REM sleep
  • Narcolepsy — Daytime drowsiness & sudden attacks of sleep; caused by loss of orexin signaling (defective orexin receptors or destruction of orexin-producing neurons)

Coma

  • Consciousness from which a person CANNOT be aroused
  • Caused by drugs, disease, or injury affecting CNS (structural lesions or diffuse disorders)
⭐ High Yield — Glasgow Coma Scale

NEED TO KNOW. 3 components: Eye Opening (4–1), Best Motor Response (6–1), Best Verbal Response (5–1). Total range 3–15.

Cerebral Blood Flow — Anatomy

  • 4 large arteries (2 carotid & 2 vertebral) merge to form the Circle of Willis — provides collateral circulation

Anterior Circulation:

Internal carotid artery→ Anterior cerebral artery→ Middle cerebral artery

Supplies frontal, parietal, lateral temporal lobes, basal ganglia, & internal capsule.

Posterior Circulation:

Vertebral arteries→ Basilar artery→ Posterior cerebral arteries

Supplies brainstem, occipital lobes, cerebellum, medial temporal lobes, & thalamus.

⭐ High Yield — Circle of Willis

KNOW THE CIRCLE OF WILLIS. 2 carotid + 2 vertebral arteries → collateral circulation to multiple brain areas.

CBF Regulation

50–65 mL/100g/min (750–900 mL/min)Normal; ~15% of cardiac output

MAP autoregulation: 60–150 mmHgCBF maintained constant in this range
⭐ High Yield — CBF Metabolic Regulators
  • ↑ CO₂ concentration → ↑ CBF (↑ 1–2 mL/100g/min [~15 mL/min] per 1 mmHg ↑ in PaCO₂)
  • ↑ Hydrogen ion (H⁺) concentration → ↑ CBF
  • ↓ O₂ concentration (PaO₂ ≤ 50 mmHg) → ↑ CBF
  • Local blood flow regulation: Astrocytes (non-neuronal cells that protect neurons & provide nutrition) release substances that can drive vasodilation (nitric oxide, adenosine, etc.)
  • CPP = MAP – ICP
  • Autoregulation: MAP 60–150 mmHg → arterial constriction if ↑CPP; dilation if ↓CPP
  • Chronic HTN → right-shifts autoregulation curve → brain vulnerable to ischemia if BP drops rapidly
  • Autoregulation impaired in: HTN, preeclampsia, atherosclerosis

Stroke / Cerebrovascular Accident

  • Caused by blocked or ruptured cerebral vessels — ischemia 88% vs hemorrhage 12%
  • Many caused by arteriosclerotic plaques that activate clotting mechanisms in the blood leading to acute loss of brain function in a localized area
⭐ High Yield — Stroke
  • Middle Cerebral Artery (MCA) — MOST COMMON STROKE SITE; left side: loss of Wernicke's (comprehension) & Broca's (word formation)
  • Posterior Cerebral Artery — Occipital pole infarction → bilateral vision loss
  • Midbrain — Lose both sensory & motor
  • TIA — Focal neurologic deficit resolving within 24 hours
  • Risk factors: HTN, smoking, hyperlipidemia, DM, EtOH, ↑ homocysteine
  • Perioperative stroke: highest risk with cardiac, neurologic, major vascular surgery; ~0.1% risk with non-CV/neuro procedures
  • Hemorrhagic types: Intraparenchymal, epidural, subdural, subarachnoid

Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF)

⭐ High Yield — CSF Facts
  • ~150 mL present total (ventricles + cisterns + subarachnoid space)
  • ~500 mL produced per day — must be absorbed at same rate
  • ~2/3 originates from choroid plexuses in 4 ventricles (mainly lateral ventricles)
  • Choroid Plexuses: Cauliflower-like growth of blood vessels covered by a layer of epithelial cells; depend mainly on active transport of Na⁺ ions
  • Function: Cushions the brain
    • Coup injury — Contusion (bruise) on same side as impact injury
    • Contrecoup injury — Contusion on the opposite side as impact injury
  • Absorption: Extra fluid empties into the venous blood through pores of arachnoidal villi
  • Pressure: ~10 mmHg / 130 mm H₂O (supine)
  • High CSF Pressure: Causes: Brain tumor, hemorrhage, infection
  • Hydrocephalus — Excessive water in the cranial vault
  • Low CSF Pressure: Caused by leaking of CSF (wet tap) → treated with epidural blood patch
  • CSF Measurement: Lie lateral (fluid pressure in spinal canal equal to cranial vault); spinal needle inserted into lumbar spinal canal (below cord); needle connected to tube open to air; spinal fluid will rise in the tube

CSF Flow Path:

Lateral ventricles→ 3rd ventricle→ Aqueduct of Sylvius→ 4th ventricle→ Foramina of Luschka (×2) & Magendie→ Cisterna magna→ Subarachnoid space

CSF vs Plasma: Osmotic pressure ≈ plasma; Na⁺ = plasma; Cl⁻ > plasma (~15%); K⁺ < plasma (~40%); Glucose < plasma (~30%)

Hydrocephalus: Noncommunicating — block in aqueduct of Sylvius; Communicating — blockage in subarachnoid spaces or at arachnoid villi.

Meningeal Layers & Spaces (Outside → In)

  • Ligamentum Flavum → Epidural space → Dura mater → Subdural space → Arachnoid Mater → Subarachnoid space (CSF) → Pia mater

Blood-Brain Barrier & Brain Edema

  • Endothelial cells joined by tight junctions (unlike other body capillaries)
  • Exist to ensure the concentrations of important constituents are maintained
  • Highly permeable: water, CO₂, O₂, lipid-soluble substances (alcohol, anesthetics)
  • Slightly permeable: electrolytes (Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺)
  • Impermeable: plasma proteins, non-lipid-soluble large organic molecules
  • Special areas with greater permeability: hypothalamus, pineal gland, area postrema
  • Types: Blood-Cerebrospinal Fluid Barrier; Blood-Brain Barrier

Brain Edema: Serious complication of abnormal cerebral fluid dynamics. Causes: capillary wall damage, head trauma, aqueductal stenosis, hydrocephalus, tumors, CPP above autoregulation upper limit. Vicious cycle: edema → ↓ blood flow → brain ischemia → arteriolar dilation (↑ capillary pressure) → more edema fluid; and separately: ↓ CBF → ↓ O₂ delivery → ↑ capillary permeability → more fluid leakage → further ↓ blood flow. Treatment: osmotic diuretic, ventricular drain, steroids, hyperventilation, surgical decompression.

Brain Metabolism

⭐ High Yield — Brain Metabolism
  • Accounts for ~15% of total body metabolism
  • NOT capable of much anaerobic metabolism — depends on continuous O₂ delivery
  • Lack of O₂ → unconsciousness within 5–10 seconds
  • Other tissues compensate anaerobically for ~30 minutes; brain cannot
  • Almost all energy from glucose derived from blood
  • ~2 min supply of glucose stored as glycogen in neurons
  • Glucose transport into neurons is NOT insulin dependent
  • Over-treating with insulin → glucose goes to insulin-sensitive cells → deficient glucose for brain

Autonomic Nervous System

General Organization

  • Controls most visceral functions of the body (continually active = 'tone')
  • Visceral Reflexes — Subconscious signals from visceral organs → autonomic ganglia/brainstem/hypothalamus → reflex responses back to organs
  • Activated mainly by: spinal cord, brain stem, & hypothalamus; portions of cerebral cortex can transmit signals

Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS)

  • Preganglionic neuron cell body: intermediolateral horn of spinal cord → ventral root → spinal nerve
  • B Fibers (myelinated preganglionic): exit CNS at thoracolumbar sites → paravertebral ganglia via white ramus → sympathetic chain → synapse in ganglia → unmyelinated postganglionic fibers → organs
  • Gray Rami: Some postganglionic fibers pass back into spinal nerves
  • Paravertebral Sympathetic Chains of Ganglia include: Celiac, Superior mesenteric, Aorticorenal, Inferior mesenteric, Hypogastric
  • Effects: Modulates activity of vascular smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, & various glands
  • "Fight or Flight Response" — Coordinates an efferent mass action response through signal amplification
⭐ High Yield — SNS Pearls
  • Stellate Ganglion — Postganglionic neurons innervate head, neck, & ipsilateral upper extremity; preganglionic from T1–T5 + superior & middle cervical ganglia; blockade used for CRPS
  • SNS Segmental Distribution (approximate rule, with overlap): T1=Head, T2=Neck, T3–T6=Thorax, T7–T11=Abdomen, T12–L2=Legs
  • Adrenal gland: Preganglionic fibers skip thoracic ganglia → go directly to adrenal medulla; chromaffin cells = postganglionic; secrete Epi (80%) + NE (20%)
  • SNS BLOCKED FIRST in neuraxial anesthesia
  • 1 preganglionic SNS fiber activates 20–30 postganglionic neurons (mass action / amplification)

Parasympathetic Nervous System (PNS)

  • Long preganglionic / Short postganglionic fibers
  • Preganglionic exit CNS at cranio-sacral sites (most pass uninterrupted to organ)
  • Cranial nerves III, VII, IX, & X + sacral nerves S1–S4
  • Unmyelinated postganglionic fibers — Usually extremely short (near or inside the innervated organs)
  • Effects: Modulates activity of vascular smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, & various glands
  • "Rest & Digest" Response — Salivation, lacrimation, urination, digestion, defecation, & sexual arousal
  • 1 preganglionic fiber → activates 1–3 postganglionic neurons (discrete, not mass action)
⭐ High Yield — PNS Cranial Nerve Distribution
  • CN X (Vagus) = 75% of all PNS — heart, lungs, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, proximal colon, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, upper ureters
  • CN III — pupillary sphincter & ciliary muscle of eye
  • CN VII — lacrimal, nasal, & submandibular glands
  • CN IX — parotid gland
  • Sacral — descending colon, rectum, urinary bladder, lower ureters, external genitalia

Acetylcholine (ACH) — Cholinergic Fibers

⭐ High Yield — ACH Release Sites & Effects
  • ACH released at: ALL preganglionic nerves (SNS & PNS), ALL postganglionic parasympathetic nerves, SOME postganglionic sympathetic nerves (sweat glands & few blood vessels), ALL somatic nerves (NMJ)
  • Enzymatic breakdown: Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)
  • DOA: a few seconds (very short)
  • CV effects: vasodilation, ↓ HR (neg chronotrope), ↓ AV conduction (neg dromotrope), ↓ contractility (neg inotrope)
  • Respiratory: bronchoconstriction, ↑ secretions
  • CNS: Role in cognitive function, motor control, appetite regulation, & nociception
  • At neuromuscular junction: Muscle contraction
  • Other: ↑ GI contractions, ↑ secretions (lacrimal, nasopharyngeal, salivary, sweat), miosis

Norepinephrine (NE) — Adrenergic Fibers

  • Released at: Most postganglionic sympathetic nerves
  • Breakdown: Reuptake into adrenergic endings (active transport); diffusion into blood; destruction by MAO & COMT
  • Synthesis: Begins in axoplasm of terminal endings; completed in secretory vesicles
  • DOA: 10–30 seconds

NE/Epi Synthesis Pathway:

Tyrosine→ DOPA→ Dopamine→ Norepinephrine→ Epinephrine

Dopamine

  • Released at: Some postganglionic sympathetic nerves
  • D1 — Blood vessels; D2 — Presynaptic sympathetic nerve terminals

ANS Anomalies

Sweat Glands: Innervated by sympathetic nerves — would expect NE. Reality: 50% release NE & 50% release ACH. Sympathetic fibers that release ACH = "cholinergic sympathetic."

Adrenal Gland: Preganglionic fibers do NOT synapse in thoracic ganglia → go directly to adrenal medulla → release ACH → chromaffin cells (postganglionic equivalent). Secrete NE (20%, 0.05 mcg/kg/min resting) + Epi (80%, 0.2 mg/kg/min resting). Epi vs NE: greater cardiac stimulation (↑ CO), weaker vasoconstriction in muscles (less ↑ BP), greater metabolic effect. DOA of adrenal-released Epi & NE: 2–4 minutes (longer than nerve-released).

Denervation

  • Intrinsic compensation develops almost to its normal basal level when sympathetic or parasympathetic tone is lost
  • Parasympathetic tone return takes longer

Denervation Supersensitivity

  • Organ sensitivity to injected NE or ACH INCREASES after denervation
  • Cause not fully known; increased postsynaptic membrane receptors ('upregulated') partially responsible

ANS Pharmacology — Drug Categories

  • Sympathomimetic/Adrenergic — Elicits same effects as sympathetic stimulation
  • Indirect Sympathomimetics — Cause release of NE from nerve endings (not direct receptor activation)
  • Parasympathomimetic/Cholinergic — Elicits same effects as parasympathetic stimulation
  • ACH Potentiators — No direct effect on effector organs; potentiate naturally secreted ACH by preventing AChE breakdown
  • Anticholinergic/Antimuscarinic — Block ACH at muscarinic receptors on effector organs
  • Adrenergic Blockers — Block adrenergic activity
  • Ganglionic Blockers — Block impulse transmission from preganglionic → postganglionic neurons

Pathophysiology — Exposure Only

📋 Exposure Only

This section is exposure-level — not heavily tested but know the terminology.

Intracranial Tumors

  • Primary tumors (gliomas) — arise from brain & coverings; classified by histologic cell type
  • Supratentorial — More common in adults; headache, seizures, new neurologic deficits
  • Infratentorial — More common in children; obstructive hydrocephalus & ataxia
  • Treatment: Surgical, chemotherapy, radiation, gamma knife irradiation, immunotherapy

Classes:

  • Astrocytoma — Most prevalent glial cell in the CNS
  • Oligodendroglioma — Arise from myelin-producing cells
  • Ependymoma — Arise from cells lining ventricles & central canal
  • Primitive Neuroectodermal Tumor — Includes retinoblastoma, medulloblastoma, pineoblastoma, neuroblastoma
  • Meningioma — Extra-axial, slow-growing, benign, from arachnoid cap cells
  • Pituitary Tumor — Arise from anterior pituitary; may occur with other endocrine tumors
  • Acoustic Neuroma — Benign schwannoma of CN VIII within internal auditory canal
  • CNS Lymphoma — Primary or metastatic from systemic lymphoma
  • Metastatic Tumor — From primary site elsewhere in body

Arteriovenous (AV) Malformation

  • Abnormal collections of blood vessels with direct arterial-to-venous connections without intervening capillaries
  • High-flow, low-resistance shunts; pressure less than systemic (believed to be congenital)

Congenital Anomalies of the Brain

  • Result from defects in development or structure of nervous system
  • Includes: Chiari malformation (congenital displacement of cerebellum), tuberous sclerosis, Von Hippel-Lindau disease, neurofibromatosis
