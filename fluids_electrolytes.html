Section 2 · Starling Forces & Fluid Exchange
Four transcapillary pressures governing fluid movement
▾

Four Transcapillary (Starling) Pressures

Fluid exchange between ECF compartments is mostly dependent on four transcapillary pressures:

↑ Absorption / Retention

  • Plasma oncotic pressure (Πp) — osmotic force of proteins within vascular space
  • Interstitial fluid pressure (Pif) — hydrostatic pressure of interstitial fluid

↑ Filtration into interstitium

  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure (Pc) — intravascular blood pressure driven by CO and vascular tone
  • Interstitial oncotic pressure (Πif) — osmotic force of proteins within interstitial space

Endothelial Glycocalyx Layer (EGL)

  • Coats the luminal surface of vascular endothelium — essential for normal fluid homeostasis
  • Highly fragile layer — can be damaged by pathophysiological conditions and interventions
  • Crystalloids freely pass through the glycocalyx
  • Colloids are held back in the vasculature by this structure
⚠ Clinical Relevance

The glycocalyx can be damaged by a number of pathophysiological conditions and interventions. Damage disrupts normal fluid homeostasis and the barrier function that retains colloids in the vasculature.

✓ Self-Check
Πp and Pif favor absorption; Pc and Πif favor filtration
Crystalloids pass through glycocalyx; colloids are held back
EGL damage → interstitial edema, impaired wound healing

Section 3 · RAAS, ADH & ANP
Hormonal regulation of sodium, water, and volume
▾

RAAS — Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

Sodium concentration is one of the primary determinants of serum osmolality and water transport. RAAS is its primary regulator.

Trigger: Hypotension (baroreceptors) + SNS stimulation
▼
Juxtaglomerular cells → Renin
▼
Renin + Angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I
▼
ACE (lungs) → Angiotensin II
▼
Angiotensin II: potent vasoconstrictor · ↑ renal Na⁺/H₂O reabsorption · stimulates aldosterone (adrenal cortex) · stimulates ADH (posterior pituitary)

ADH — Antidiuretic Hormone

  • Primarily regulates water balance
  • Released from posterior pituitary in response to ↑ serum osmolality (detected by hypothalamic osmoreceptors)
  • Acts on aquaporin channels in kidney → reabsorbs water → ↑ circulating volume
  • Also acts as a potent arterial vasoconstrictor when released in response to ↓ blood volume (baroreceptors)
  • Hypothalamus also stimulates thirst in response to ↑ serum osmolality

ANP — Atrial Natriuretic Peptide

  • Stretch receptors in atrial walls stimulate ANP release from myocytes in response to ↑ preload / hypervolemic states
  • ANP → ↑ renal removal of Na⁺ and water; ↑ GFR; inhibits renin and ADH release
  • When preload is low → atrial receptors inhibit ANP release
💡 Clinical Pearl

Laparoscopic pneumoperitoneum: mechanical blood shunting into thorax → ↑ CVP → stimulates ANP release. Also activates RAAS via ↓ renal perfusion.

✓ Self-Check
RAAS pathway: hypotension → renin → Ang I → ACE → Ang II → aldosterone + ADH
ADH: osmoreceptors detect ↑ osmolality → aquaporins → H₂O reabsorption
ANP: released with ↑ atrial stretch (hypervolemia) → promotes Na⁺/H₂O excretion
ANP inhibits both renin and ADH release

Phase 2

Fluid Types — Crystalloids & Colloids

4
Section 4 · Crystalloids — Overview & Comparison
NS, LR, Plasmalyte — properties, indications, and pitfalls
▾

Crystalloids contain ion salts and other low-molecular weight substances. Preferred for dehydration / plasma hypertonicity — contribute to hydration of the entire ECV. Due to low molecular weight, ~80% distributes into the interstitial space rather than staying intravascular.

Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl)

  • Roughly equal concentrations of Na⁺ and Cl⁻
  • Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and marked base excess alterations from chloride load
  • May exacerbate volume overload if using negative base excess as a trigger for infusion therapy
  • Hyperchloremia → ↓ GFR and impairs renal bicarbonate handling
  • Increased Na⁺ load → water retention, hemodilution, interstitial edema, negative bowel motility effects
⚠ Bottom Line

Of all isotonic solutions, NS is the least physiologic but most commonly administered.

Lactated Ringer's (LR)

  • Contains sodium lactate as a bicarbonate substrate / buffering agent
  • More effective than NS at preserving intravascular volume
  • Not recommended for large-volume administration in diabetics — lactate metabolites are gluconeogenic
  • Contraindicated in TBI and neurovascular insults — slight hypotonicity may promote cerebral edema / hypo-osmolality
  • Contains calcium → contraindicated for co-administration with citrated blood products (risk of coagulation)

Plasmalyte / Normosol-R / Isolyte-S

💡 Most Physiologic Crystalloid
  • Most isotonic of the balanced salt solutions; most favorable acid-base profile
  • Uses sodium gluconate + sodium acetate as alkalinizing buffers (not lactate)
  • No calcium → compatible with blood products
  • Better renal function and perfusion preservation than NS
  • Patients receiving these have less acid-base alteration and require fewer blood product transfusions vs. nonbuffered solutions
✓ Self-Check
~80% of crystalloid distributes to the interstitial space
NS → hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis; least physiologic of the isotonics
LR: contraindicated in TBI (hypo-osmolality) and with citrated blood products (calcium)
Plasmalyte: no calcium, most physiologic balanced salt solution — compatible with blood products

Next — macromolecular solutions that stay in the vascular space…

Section 5 · Colloids — HES & Albumin
Macromolecules that expand plasma oncotic pressure
▾

Colloids contain macromolecules suspended in electrolyte solutions that promote volume expansion by directly increasing plasma oncotic pressure (Πp). Held back by the intact glycocalyx.

Hydroxyethyl Starches (HES)

  • Derived from starchy plants — have allergenic potential
  • Dose-dependent coagulopathy from: hemodilution, binding of clotting factors, interference with platelet adhesion, alterations in plasma viscosity
  • Can form interstitial colloid deposits → severe non-histamine–related pruritus and nephrotoxicity
⚠ Black Box Warning

HES associated with renal injury and increased mortality. Best avoided in: pre-existing renal injury · sepsis · neurologic injury · organ donors · CABG with CPB (excess bleeding).

Albumin

  • Produced from human blood, heat-treated to remove pathogens, suspended in saline
  • 0.011% risk of anaphylactoid reactions
  • Used as a volume expander in active blood loss not requiring transfusion
  • Carrier for protein-bound drugs, metabolites, electrolytes, enzymes, hormones
  • Administration advised in hypoalbuminemia
  • May be the preferred solution for intravascular replacement in patients with an intact glycocalyx during acute volume loss
✓ Self-Check
Colloids expand plasma volume by increasing oncotic pressure (Πp)
HES: Black Box Warning — renal injury, ↑ mortality; avoid in sepsis, renal failure, CPB
Albumin: 0.011% anaphylactoid risk; preferred in intact-glycocalyx acute volume loss

Surgical Context — Traditional Methods & Their Limitations

Section 6 · Surgical Stress Response on Fluid Balance
HPA axis, catecholamines, and laparoscopic considerations
▾

HPA Axis Activation

Somatic and autonomic nerve stimulation at the surgical incision activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis:

Surgical incision → nerve stimulation
▼
Hypothalamus releases CRH
▼
Anterior pituitary secretes ACTH
▼
Adrenal cortex releases Cortisol: protein catabolism · hepatic gluconeogenesis & glycogenolysis · ↑ plasma proteins
Unchecked hyperglycemia → disrupts glycocalyx + impairs wound healing

SNS / Catecholamine Response

  • Catecholamines released from adrenal medulla: ↑ HR, vasoconstriction, ↑ metabolic rate, ↑ O₂ demand
  • ↑ ADH release, K⁺ excretion, water reabsorption
  • Surgical trauma / tissue injury → endothelial release of inflammatory mediators → hyperthermia, ↑ O₂ demand, altered microcirculatory flow

Laparoscopic / Pneumoperitoneum Effects

  • ↓ Tissue damage cytokine release (benefit vs. open surgery)
  • Mechanical suppression of splanchnic blood flow → ischemia → predisposes to reperfusion injury
  • ↑ CVP from blood shunting into thorax → stimulates ANP release
  • ↑ Afterload, ↑ MAP, ↓ EF; ↓ renal perfusion → activates RAAS
  • Hypercarbia from CO₂ absorption → acidosis → myocardial depression; mitigate with ↑ minute ventilation
✓ Self-Check
HPA pathway: incision → CRH → ACTH → cortisol → gluconeogenesis + ↑ plasma proteins
Hyperglycemia from unchecked cortisol → disrupts glycocalyx + impairs wound healing
Pneumoperitoneum: ↓ splanchnic flow, ↑ CVP (ANP), ↑ afterload, activates RAAS, hypercarbia

Section 7 · Traditional Fluid Methods & Why They Fail
4-2-1 rule, surgical guidelines, and the evidence against them
▾

4-2-1 Maintenance Rule

WeightHourly Rate
0–10 kg4 mL/kg/hr
11–20 kg4 mL/kg/hr (first 10 kg) + 2 mL/kg/hr (every kg >10)
>20 kg4 mL/kg/hr (first 10) + 2 mL/kg/hr (next 10) + 1 mL/kg/hr (every kg >20)

Estimated Deficit = Maintenance requirement × Fasting hours
Replacement: ½ in 1st hour · ¼ in 2nd hr · ¼ in 3rd hr

Traditional Surgical Replacement Guidelines

TypeRate
Superficial trauma1–2 mL/kg/hr
Minimal trauma2–4 mL/kg/hr
Moderate trauma4–6 mL/kg/hr
Blood loss replacement — Crystalloid3 mL for every 1 mL EBL
Blood loss replacement — Colloid1 mL for every 1 mL EBL

Problems with Traditional Methods

⚠ Why Traditional Methods Are Insufficient
  • Studies show relatively healthy fasted patients maintain normal intravascular volumes — prophylactic volume administration → glycocalyx disruption + fluid overload
  • Preoperative deficits can still exist (especially with bowel preps)
  • Evaporative losses are likely overestimated — animal studies show exteriorized bowel losses plateau at 1 mL/kg/hr
  • The "third space" concept is questionable — studies support some uncounted ECV loss but cannot identify the actual location
  • Traditional methods rely on nonspecific indicators: MAP, CVP, urine output
  • CVP altered by many states — only ~50% of patients with low CVP are fluid responsive
  • Urine output unreliable — affected by neuroendocrine response, ADH levels
  • Patients with high ADH levels can retain administered fluid → expansion of interstitial space
  • Gross variability in defining "liberal" vs. "restrictive" therapy → no conclusive best-practice results

Complications of Inappropriate Fluid Therapy

Under-Resuscitation

  • Hypovolemia
  • ↓ Perfusion / O₂ delivery
  • PONV
  • Renal dysfunction
  • Myocardial ischemia
  • Hemoconcentration / thrombosis

Over-Resuscitation

  • Vascular overload
  • Microvascular congestion / ↓ O₂ delivery
  • Glycocalyx damage
  • Hypo-coagulation
  • Anemia, thrombocytopenia
  • Poor wound healing
  • ↓ Organ perfusion & congestion
  • ↑ EVLWI · Compartment syndrome
✓ Self-Check
4-2-1 rule: 4 mL/kg/hr (0–10 kg) + 2 mL/kg/hr (11–20 kg) + 1 mL/kg/hr (>20 kg)
Estimated deficit = maintenance rate × fasting hours; give ½ in 1st hr, ¼ + ¼ after
CVP unreliable: only ~50% of patients with low CVP are fluid responsive
Urine output unreliable — affected by ADH and neuroendocrine response to surgery

Phase 4

Goal-Directed Fluid Therapy (GDFT)

8
Section 8 · GDFT — Principles, Outcomes & Frank-Starling
Individualized hemodynamic endpoints and the physiologic foundation
▾
⭐ Definition

GDFT utilizes individualized hemodynamic end-points monitored by hemodynamic modalities to support oxygen transport balance, minimize O₂ demand, optimize CO, tissue oxygenation, capillary and microvascular flow, and end-organ perfusion.

Outcomes with GDFT

  • ↓ Infection
  • ↓ Duration of mechanical ventilation & ICU stay
  • ↓ PONV, coagulopathy, hypotension, anemia
  • ↓ Respiratory and cardiovascular complications
  • ↓ 30-day mortality
  • ↓ Renal impairment

Frank-Starling Mechanism — The Physiologic Foundation

Describes the relationship between LVEDV and myocardial contractility (stroke volume):

  • Ascending portion of the curve = preload dependence — fluid challenges will ↑ SV
  • Plateau portion = preload independence — further fluid will NOT ↑ SV and may harm
  • This is why GDFT is vital: it focuses interventions to individual cardiovascular status
  • Patients on a normal curve → respond to ↑ fluid volume with ↑ contractility
  • Patients on a pathophysiologic curve → can only respond to small volumes without compromise
✓ Self-Check
GDFT: individualized hemodynamic endpoints to optimize O₂ delivery and end-organ perfusion
Frank-Starling: ascending = preload dependent; plateau = preload independent
On pathophysiologic curve: only small volumes tolerated before compromise

Section 9 · GDFT Monitoring Techniques
Dilution, pulse contour, Doppler, TEE, tissue oxygenation
▾

Dilution Techniques

Administer a fixed volume of injectate into the vascular system; measure CO based on the area under a time-temperature or concentration-time curve.

Pulse Contour Analysis

  • Measures preload responsiveness by quantifying changes in arterial, capnography, or pulse oximetry waveforms
  • Patient considered fluid responsive if calculated value >13–15%

Esophageal Doppler & Echocardiography

  • Esophageal Doppler: ultrasound technology to monitor LV function and aortic compliance
  • TEE = gold standard for direct cardiac function and volume status monitoring

Measures of Tissue Oxygenation

Measure global tissue oxygen balance by comparing blood gases with oxygen consumption and delivery.

✓ Self-Check

Electrolyte Imbalances — Na, K, Ca, Phosphate, Mg

10
Section 10 · Sodium Imbalance
Hypo- and hypernatremia — classification, symptoms, correction rates
▾
⭐ Key Physiology
  • Na⁺ is responsible for the majority of osmotic activity in the ECF
  • Water moves from lower → higher solute concentration to maintain equilibrium
  • Na⁺ imbalances reflect an impaired concentration between water and sodium
  • Treatment: restrict or expand water AND supplement or eliminate sodium

Hyponatremia

ECV is hypo-osmolar compared to ICV → water moves into ICV. Most significant effect is cerebral edema (brain confined within fixed cranium).

Symptoms by Plasma Na⁺ Level

Treatment

⚠ Correction Rate — Critical
  • Initial: fluid restriction, diuresis, ± vasopressin receptor antagonists (tolvaptan, conivaptan)
  • Treat with 3% saline at 1–2 mL/kg/hr
  • Correct no more than 1–2 mEq/L/hr
  • Raise no more than 10–15 mmol/L in 24 hours
  • Rapid correction → Central Pontine Myelinolysis (osmotic demyelination)

Hypernatremia

Usually the result of impaired water intake. Rapidly occurring hypernatremia → rapid brain shrinkage → traction on intracranial veins → intracranial hemorrhage. Rapid treatment with free water → risk of cerebral edema.

Free Water Deficit: [(Na/140) – 1] × TBW

Section 11 · Potassium Imbalance
Hypo- and hyperkalemia — ECG changes, causes, and treatment
▾
⭐ Key Numbers
  • Principle intracellular electrolyte — 98% of body's K⁺ storage
  • ICF: 150–160 mEq/L  |  ECF: 3.5–5 mEq/L
  • Aldosterone stimulates K⁺ excretion
  • K⁺ >5.5 or <4 mEq/L undergoing noncardiac surgery → ↑ risk of MACE

Hypokalemia — Serum <3.5 mEq/L

Symptoms

  • Mild: palpitations, skeletal muscle weakness & pain
  • Serum <2.5: paresthesias, depressed DTR, fasciculations, weakness, altered LOC
  • Arrhythmias: 1st & 2nd degree block, atrial or V-fib, asystole

Causes

CategoryExamples
Renal lossesDiuretics, steroids, metabolic acidosis, hyperaldosteronism, DKA, alcohol
Non-renal lossesSweating, diarrhea, vomiting, laxatives
↓ IntakeEthanol, malnutrition
Intracellular shiftHyperventilation, metabolic alkalosis, beta-agonists, theophylline
EndocrinopathyCushing disease, Bartter syndrome, insulin therapy
💡 Treatment
  • 10–20 mEq/hr KCl IV (dextrose-free); oral therapy once stabilized
  • Hypochloremia makes it difficult for the kidney to conserve K⁺
  • Insulin → facilitates K⁺ redistribution into intracellular space

Hyperkalemia — Serum >5 mEq/L

⚠ ECG Warning

When K⁺ >8 mEq/L → QRS widening → Sine waves → V-fib → Cardiac arrest
Always rule out pseudohyperkalemia (hemolysis, leukocytosis, thrombosis, prolonged fist clenching during draw)

Section 12 · Calcium Imbalance
Hypo- and hypercalcemia — causes, symptoms, and treatment
▾
⭐ Key Numbers
  • 99% in bones as hydroxyapatite; 1% in plasma and cells
  • Total serum calcium: 8.5–10.5 mg/dL
  • Normal ionized values: 2–2.5 mEq/L
  • Forms: ionized + physiologically active (50%) · bound to anions (10%) · bound to proteins (40%)
  • Primarily maintained by PTH (parathyroid hormone)
  • Measure ionized calcium — preferred, especially in critical illness or hypoalbuminemia
  • Acidemia → ↓ protein-bound → ↑ ionized portion (and vice versa)
  • Second messenger in muscle contraction, neurotransmitter/hormone release, coagulation, myocardial contractility

Hypocalcemia

⚠ Most Likely Intraoperative Causes

Hyperventilation and rapid massive transfusion (citrate binding)

Causes

  • PTH insufficiency: primary (congenital syndromes); secondary (neck surgery, metastatic carcinoma, hypo/hypermagnesemia, sepsis, pancreatitis, burns, ethanol, cimetidine)
  • Vitamin D insufficiency: rickets, malnutrition, malabsorption, liver/renal disease, hypomagnesemia, anticonvulsants (phenytoin, primidone)
  • PTH resistance / Calcium chelation: hyperphosphatemia, citrate, free fatty acids, alkalosis, fluoride poisoning

Symptoms

SystemSymptoms
NeuromuscularParesthesia, weakness, spasm, tetany, Chvostek's sign (facial/eye twitching), Trousseau's sign (carpopedal spasm), hyperreflexia, seizures
CardiovascularBradycardia, angina, hypotension, CHF, cardiac arrest, digitalis insensitivity, QT prolongation
PulmonaryBronchospasm, laryngospasm
PsychiatricAnxiety, depression, irritability, confusion, psychosis, dementia
💡 Treatment
  • Guide by ionized calcium levels
  • 10 mL Calcium gluconate = 93 mg elemental calcium (peripheral-friendly)
  • Calcium chloride = 272 mg elemental calcium — most rapid, but tissue necrosis and venous irritation risk

Hypercalcemia

Causes

  • Primary hyperparathyroidism
  • Malignant disease: PTHrP, ectopic 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, osteolytic metastasis
  • Medications: thiazide diuretics, lithium, estrogens, vitamin D/A toxicity, calcium ingestion
  • Granulomatous disorders: sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, coccidioidomycosis, berylliosis, histoplasmosis, leprosy
  • Nonparathyroid endocrine disorders: hyperparathyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, pheochromocytoma, acromegaly, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide-producing tumor
  • Miscellaneous: milk-alkali syndrome, immobilization

Symptoms

SystemSymptoms
NeurologicFatigue, weakness, confusion, lethargy, seizures, coma, hypotonia, ↓ DTR
CardiovascularHypertension, ST elevation, sinus brady/arrest, AV block, short QT, BBB, ventricular dysrhythmias, potentiation of digoxin toxicity
RenalPolyuria, polydipsia, dehydration, electrolyte loss, prerenal azotemia, nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis
GINausea/vomiting, anorexia, peptic ulcers, pancreatitis, constipation/ileus
💡 Treatment
  • NS infusion → increases Ca²⁺ excretion
  • Add loop diuretic to further enhance excretion
  • Also: bisphosphonates, mithramycin, calcitonin, glucocorticoids, phosphate salts
  • Hemodialysis for acute life-threatening hypercalcemia

Section 13 · Phosphate & Magnesium Imbalances
Hypo/hyperphosphatemia and hypo/hypermagnesemia — clinical uses of Mg
▾

Phosphate

  • 85% in bone; plasma forms: phospholipids, phosphate esters, inorganic phosphate (ionized)
  • Intracellular roles: component of ATP and 2,3-DPG
  • Acts as a buffer in acid-base balance
  • Concentration inversely proportional to calcium; regulated by same mechanisms (PTH, vitamin D, calcitonin)

Magnesium — Normal Range: 1.5–3 mEq/L

Regulated in intestines and kidneys. Functions in enzymatic processes, Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, protein synthesis, neuromuscular excitability. Common in hospitalized patients, critical illness, and excessive alcohol use.

Hypomagnesemia

Symptoms: Inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → ↓ ICV K⁺; N/V, tetany, flat T-waves, U-waves, prolonged QT, wide QRS, arrhythmias
