Section 1 · Asthma

Asthma — Pathophysiology & Triggers

What's happening in the airway and what sets it off.
+

Definition & Mechanism

  • Reversible airflow obstruction + chronic airway inflammation + bronchial hyper-reactivity + bronchoconstriction.
  • Inflammatory cascade → infiltration with neutrophils, mast cells, T-cells, eosinophils, leukotrienes → airway edema (especially bronchi).
  • Thickening of basement membrane and airway wall also occurs.
  • Main inflammatory mediators: histamine, prostaglandin D2, leukotrienes.
  • Cause is multifactorial: environmental + genetic. Risk factors include family history, maternal smoking in pregnancy, frequent viral infections, limited early allergen exposure.

Clinical Presentation & Triggers

  • Episodic disease with acute exacerbations: dyspnea, eosinophilia, cough, tachypnea, hyperventilation, wheezing, chest tightness.
  • Attacks provoked by: allergens, infections, stress, exercise, and drugs — ASA, beta-antagonists, NSAIDs.
  • Mild asthma typically accompanied by normal PaO2 and PaCO2.
Two-Category Treatment Framework 1 — Control: corticosteroids + antileukotrienes (suppress the inflammation). 2 — Rescue: beta-2 agonists (reverse the bronchoconstriction fast).

Section 2 · Asthma in the OR

Perioperative Asthma Management

Pre-op planning, induction strategy, and what to do when bronchospasm happens anyway.
+

Pre-op

  • Goal: formulate a plan that prevents or blunts expiratory airflow obstruction.
  • Continue anti-inflammatory and bronchodilator therapy up to induction.
  • If steroid use >6 months → consider stress-dose hydrocortisone or methylprednisolone.
  • Regional anesthesia may be more advantageous than GA.

Induction & Maintenance

  • Suppress airway reflexes to avoid bronchoconstriction: Propofol, Ketamine, Opioid, Lidocaine, Volatile agents.
  • LMA is less likely to cause bronchoconstriction than ETT.
  • Patient may benefit from increased expiratory time.

Intraoperative Bronchospasm

  • Incidence: 0.2–4.2% of all procedures in asthma patients.
  • Signs: wheezing, ↑ peak pressure, ↓ exhaled TV, slowly rising ETCO2 waveform.
  • First response: deepen anesthesia with a volatile agent.
  • If persists: beta-2 agonist + corticosteroid.
  • Refractory: Aminophylline.

Status Asthmaticus

Crisis — life-threatening bronchospasm unresponsive to treatment Continued resistance may indicate mucous plugging or severe edema.
Treatment: inhaled beta-2 agonists (intermittent or continuous) + supplemental O2 to keep sats >90%.
IV corticosteroids early (effects take hours): Cortisol 2 mg/kg + 0.5 mg/kg/hr OR Methylprednisolone 60–125 mg IV Q6.
PaCO2 >50 despite therapy → intubation + mechanical ventilation.
May require GA to produce bronchodilation.

Section 3 · COPD

COPD — Pathophysiology, Diagnosis & Treatment

Chronic bronchitis vs. emphysema, risk factors, and the key PaO2 thresholds.
+

Definition

  • Consists of chronic bronchitis and emphysema.
  • NOT fully reversible — distinguishes it from asthma.
  • Mechanisms: ↓ lung elasticity & recoil, ↓ bronchiolar wall rigidity → collapse on exhalation, bronchospasm from secretions, enlargement of air sacs (emphysema).

Chronic Bronchitis vs. Emphysema

FeatureChronic BronchitisEmphysema
Main symptomChronic productive coughDyspnea
Obstruction mechanism↓ lumen diameter from mucus & inflammationLoss of elastic recoil
CXR finding—Bullae = emphysema diagnosis
  • Hematocrit tends to increase in COPD to compensate for ↓ PaO2.

Risk Factors & Alpha-1 Antitrypsin

  • Smoking, frequent respiratory infections, occupational dust (mining, textiles), pollution, genetic: alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (rare homozygous condition).
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin protects elastic lung tissue from neutrophil elastase. Defective form accumulates in liver → cirrhosis; absent normal form in lungs → COPD/asthma.
O2 thresholds — memorize Supplement O2 if PaO2 < 55 mmHg · Goal: maintain > 60 mmHg

Medical Treatment

  • Smoking cessation + supplemental O2 (most important).
  • Drug therapy: long-acting beta-2 agonists (LABA) + inhaled corticosteroids + long-acting anticholinergics (LAMA).
  • Cor pulmonale / right heart disease → add diuretics.
  • Exacerbations: systemic corticosteroids ± antibiotics.

Section 4 · COPD in the OR

Anesthetic Management of COPD

Volatile agents, ventilator settings, and what to avoid.
+
  • Regional suitable for lower intra-abdominal and lower extremity procedures — as long as large sedative doses not needed.
  • Volatile agents preferred: rapid elimination + bronchodilation.
    • Avoid nitrous oxide in emphysema with known bullae.
    • Volatiles can attenuate HPV → may need to ↑ FiO2.
  • Opioids may be less useful due to prolonged ventilatory depression.
Ventilator strategy TV 6–8 ml/kg · RR 6–10/min · ↓ I:E ratio
Slow rate + reduced I:E = adequate time for exhalation, reduced air trapping.

Section 5 · The Framework

ANS Influence on the Airway

PSNS constricts, beta-2 dilates — the receptor logic behind every drug class.
+
SystemReceptorMediatorEffect
PSNSMuscarinic M3Acetylcholine (via vagus)Bronchoconstriction, ↑ glandular activity
SNSBeta-2 (smooth muscle)CatecholaminesBronchodilation via G-protein → cAMP
  • PSNS also controls airway microvasculature and caliber; preganglionic fibers via vagus nerve synapse with postganglionic fibers in airway parasympathetic ganglia.
  • SNS has no direct control over airway tone — effect is mediated entirely through beta-2 receptors.
The drug logic Block PSNS (M3) → less bronchoconstriction = anticholinergics.
Stimulate SNS (B2) → bronchodilation = beta-2 agonists.
Suppress inflammation → corticosteroids, antileukotrienes, mast cell stabilizers.

Section 6 · Beta-2 Agonists (Inhaled)

Inhaled Adrenergic Agonists

SABA vs. LABA, the B2→cAMP cascade, and what happens to 80–90% of the dose.
+

Mechanism

Drug binds B2 receptor→ G-protein activates adenylate cyclase→ ATP → cAMP→ Inactivates myosin light chain kinase + ↓ intracellular Ca²⁺→ Smooth muscle relaxation

SABA vs. LABA

Short-Acting (SABA)Long-Acting (LABA)
Onset<5 min5–10 min
Duration3–4 hours12 hours
UseRapid rescue: wheezing, bronchospasmMaintenance, ↓ exacerbations
ExamplesAlbuterol, Levalbuterol, Metaproterenol, PirbuterolSalmeterol, Formoterol, Arformoterol
Deposition pearl 80–90% of the total dose is deposited in the mouth or pharynx — not the lungs. To improve bronchial deposition: slow inhalation + breath-hold ~5 seconds at end of inspiration.
  • Most preparations are mixtures of R and S isomers.
  • Side effects: tremors & tachycardia (B2 stimulation in skeletal muscle/vasculature), hyperglycemia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, tolerance, temporary ↓ arterial O2 tension.

Section 7 · Beta-2 Agonists (Systemic)

Systemic Adrenergic Agonists

Reserved for rescue; same mechanism, more systemic exposure.
+
  • Reserved for rescue therapy; same mechanism of action as inhaled agents.
  • Terbutaline: available oral, subcutaneous, IV.
  • Epinephrine: available IV & SQ.
    • Rapid, effective bronchodilator: 0.4 ml of 1 mg/ml SQ or nebulized 0.5 ml 2.25% solution diluted to 3 ml with NS.
    • Maximal bronchodilation at 15 min, duration 1 hour.
  • Side effect profile similar to inhaled agents — tremor and tachycardia most common.

Inhaled Cholinergic Antagonists

Ipratropium vs. tiotropium, the M1/M2/M3 receptor roles, and the contraction cascade.
+

Mechanism — The Three Muscarinic Receptors

ReceptorEffect
M1Bronchoconstriction + mucus production
M2Limits ACh production — protective against bronchoconstriction
M3Bronchoconstriction + mucus production
ACh binds M1/M3→ G-protein or ↑ cGMP→ Phospholipase C → IP3→ ↑ Intracellular Ca²⁺→ Myosin light chain kinase → contraction

Ipratropium vs. Tiotropium

DrugUseDuration
IpratropiumMaintenance AND rescue for COPD; asthma during acute exacerbationsShort-acting
TiotropiumMaintenance only (COPD + approved for asthma maintenance)Long-acting
  • Primarily for COPD — not usually recommended in asthma except acute exacerbations.
  • Benefits: ↑ exercise tolerance, ↓ dyspnea, ↑ gas exchange, ↓ exacerbations.
  • Side effects: dry mouth, urinary retention, pupil dilation, blurred vision.

Section 11 · ICS

Inhaled Corticosteroids

First-line controller for asthma; adjunct only in moderate-severe COPD.
+
  • Mechanism: target glucocorticoid receptor in the cytoplasm of airway epithelial cells → ↓ inflammatory changes, ↑ lung function, ↓ exacerbations.
  • Used as multimodal therapy when attacks increase in severity or frequency.
  • In COPD: only recommended in moderate-to-severe disease, combined with LABA.

Examples (Monotherapy)

  • Beclomethasone, Budesonide, Ciclesonide, Flunisolide, Fluticasone, Mometasone, Triamcinolone.
  • Combination: Budesonide/Formoterol, Fluticasone/Salmeterol.
Side effect list — know them ↑ pneumonia, oropharyngeal candidiasis, pharyngitis, easy bruising, cataracts, ↑ IOP, osteoporosis, dysphonia, cough, minor growth reduction in children.

Section 12 · Systemic Corticosteroids

Systemic Corticosteroids (Oral & IV)

Exacerbation rescue — doses, duration limits, and the side effect burden.
+
  • Used for COPD and asthma exacerbations. In asthma: only if severe or not responsive to beta-2 agents.
  • Some severe-disease patients may be on chronic oral corticosteroids.
Doses Prednisone 30–60 mg PO per dose
Methylprednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg IV Q6–12
Duration: do not exceed 2 weeks.
  • Side effects: hypertension, dermal thinning, ↑ infections, cataracts, adrenal suppression, psychosis, peptic ulcers, hyperglycemia.

Section 13 · Leukotriene Modifiers

Leukotriene Modifiers

Asthma-only long-term control; two classes, one liver-monitoring drug.
+
  • Only used in asthma for long-term control as multimodal therapy with beta agonists ± ICS.
  • Two classes: leukotriene receptor antagonists (Montelukast, Zafirlukast, Pranlukast) and leukotriene inhibitors (Zileuton).
Arachidonic acid→ 5-lipoxygenase pathway→ Leukotrienes → bronchoconstriction, edema, ↑ secretions, eosinophil migration
  • Taken PO; bronchodilation within hours; maximal effect in a few days.
  • Generally well tolerated. Exception: Zileuton → reversible hepatitis in 2–4% → periodic liver labs required.

Section 14 · Mast Cell Stabilizers

Mast Cell Stabilizers

Prevention before exposure — minimal side effects, great for exercise-induced or allergen-triggered asthma.
+
  • Cromolyn sodium and nedocromil — delivered by powder inhaler or nebulizer.
  • Stabilize submucosal and intraluminal mast cells by interfering with antigen-dependent release of mediators responsible for bronchoconstriction, edema, and mucus secretion.
  • Can be used as preventative treatment prior to known allergen exposure or exercise.
  • No major side effects; minor: GI upset, coughing, throat irritation.

Section 15 · Methylxanthines

Methylxanthines (Theophylline / Aminophylline)

The alternative bronchodilator with a narrow therapeutic index — monitor levels.
+
  • Only recommended as an alternative treatment for COPD and asthma.
  • Dual mechanism: non-selective phosphodiesterase inhibitor → ↑ cAMP & cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation; and adenosine receptor antagonist → inhibits histamine & leukotriene release from mast cells + anti-inflammatory effects.
Narrow therapeutic index — blood levels monitored Significant side effects at blood levels >20 mg/L: headache, nausea, vomiting, diuresis, reflux, abdominal discomfort, restlessness, seizures, cardiac arrhythmias, death.

Inhaled Nitric Oxide (iNO)

  • Selectively delivered to ventilated lung → ↑ alveolar perfusion, ↓ intrapulmonary shunt.
  • Mechanism: activates guanylyl cyclase → GTP → cGMP → vasodilation.
  • Only FDA-approved for infants with respiratory distress syndrome → reduces need for ECMO.
  • Monitor methemoglobin levels when NO given >24 hours.
  • Also has antithrombotic effects.

Prostaglandins

  • Prostanoids: vascular relaxation, inhibit smooth muscle cell proliferation, inhibit platelet aggregation → risk of intraoperative/postoperative bleeding.
  • Epoprostenol: must be delivered continuously. Treprostinil & Iloprost: can be given intermittently.
  • May cause temporary ↓ PaO2 during one-lung ventilation.

Section 19 · The Lung as an Organ of Drug Metabolism

Intrinsic Pharmacologic Lung Effects

Pulmonary first-pass, ACE, RAAS, and eicosanoid metabolism — the lung does far more than gas exchange.

Pulmonary Uptake & First-Pass

  • Pulmonary uptake = drug transferred blood → lung (doesn't indicate if metabolized or returned to blood).
  • First-pass uptake = amount removed during blood's first pass through the lungs.
DrugFirst-Pass Pulmonary Uptake
Fentanyl90%
Lidocaine50%
Sufentanil45%
Propofol30%
Thiopental15%
Morphine / Ketamine10%
  • Lungs have considerable P450 enzymes (especially in pneumocytes) → responsible for some degree of metabolism.

The Lung & RAAS

Kidney releases renin→ Angiotensinogen → Angiotensin I→ Pulmonary ACE converts Ang I → Ang II
  • Pulmonary endothelium has high concentrations of ACE — major role in RAAS.
  • Bradykinin (bronchoconstrictor, antithrombotic, profibrinolytic) is metabolized by ACE.
  • Eicosanoid (arachidonic acid derivative) production and metabolism also occur in the lungs via lipoxygenase, cyclooxygenase, or P450 monooxygenase systems.
